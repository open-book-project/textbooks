    About This Book¶

    Who it is for and how to use it

    Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds: the fuels we burn, the plastics we wrap things in, the medicines we take, the dyes in our clothes and the molecules of life itself. It has a reputation for being a subject of endless memorisation. It is not. A small number of ideas (where the electrons are, how atoms share them, and how they move when molecules meet) explain nearly every reaction in this book. Learn those ideas properly and the reactions stop being a list and start being a story.

    Who this book is for¶

    This book is a first course in organic chemistry. It is written for a student in the last years of school or the first year of a university course in chemistry, biology, pharmacy, medicine, chemical engineering or a related subject, and for anyone teaching themselves.

    You need school chemistry: atoms with protons, neutrons and electrons, the periodic table, the idea of a chemical bond, balanced equations, moles, and a first acquaintance with acids, bases and equilibrium. Nothing more is assumed. Where a piece of general chemistry is needed (electron shells, electronegativity, logarithms in pH) it is recalled at the point of use.

    What the book covers¶

    The sixteen chapters fall into four groups.

    Part Chapters What you learn
    Foundations 1 to 5 Bonding and shape, drawing and naming molecules, isomerism and stereochemistry, acids and bases, and the language of mechanisms (curly arrows)
    Hydrocarbons 6 to 8 Alkanes and their conformations, the addition reactions of alkenes and alkynes, and the special stability and substitution chemistry of benzene
    Functional groups 9 to 13 Alkyl halides (substitution and elimination), alcohols and ethers, aldehydes and ketones, carboxylic acids and their derivatives, and amines
    Tools and applications 14 to 16 Spectroscopy (IR, NMR and mass spectrometry), polymers and the molecules of life, and safe and sustainable practice in the laboratory

    Table P.1. The plan of the book.

    The order matters. Chapters 1 to 5 build the toolkit used everywhere else: by the end of Chapter 5 you will be able to read and draw a reaction mechanism. Chapters 6 to 13 then apply the same few mechanistic patterns to one family of compounds after another. Chapter 14 shows how chemists find out what they have made, and Chapters 15 and 16 connect the subject to materials, biology and the practice of the laboratory.

    How each chapter is built¶

    Every chapter has the same parts, so you always know where you are.

    • Learning outcomes at the top say what you should be able to do at the end.
    • Numbered sections explain the ideas, moving from a picture to a precise statement.
    • Figures show structures and mechanisms. Structures are drawn as skeletal formulas (Chapter 1 explains how to read them), and curly arrows in mechanisms are drawn in red.
    • Boxes pick out what matters: a key idea box holds the one thing to remember from a section, a note adds detail, and a caution box marks a common mistake.
    • Worked examples solve a problem step by step.
    • A summary lists the main points.
    • Exercises follow, each with its answer folded underneath. Try the exercise on paper before you open the answer.
    • A short quiz at the end checks your understanding. You can answer it on the page and see at once whether you were right.

    At the back are a glossary of the terms used in the book and a list of further reading.

    How to use the book¶

    Organic chemistry is learned with a pencil. Read a section, then close the book and draw the structures and mechanisms yourself. When a mechanism appears, do not try to memorise the picture: ask, for each arrow, where the electrons come from and why they go where they go. If you can answer that question, you will be able to work out mechanisms you have never seen.

    Three habits will help more than anything else:

    1. Count electrons and check charges. Every structure you draw should have the right number of bonds to each atom and the right formal charges. Most errors in mechanisms are errors of bookkeeping.
    2. Draw molecules as skeletal formulas. They are faster, clearer and closer to how chemists think. Chapter 1 shows you how.
    3. Look for patterns. A nucleophile attacking a carbonyl group in Chapter 11 is the same event as a nucleophile attacking an alkyl halide in Chapter 9, seen in a different setting. The book points these links out; notice them.

    Safety first

    This book describes reactions to explain how molecules behave. It is not a laboratory manual. Laboratory work must be done only under supervision, in a properly equipped laboratory, following a risk assessment and the local safety rules. Chapter 16 explains the principles of safe practice.

    Conventions¶

    Names follow the current IUPAC recommendations (propan-2-ol, ethanoic acid), with common names given where they are still widely used (isopropyl alcohol, acetic acid). Formulas are written with subscripts (CH₃CH₂OH). Energies are in kilojoules per mole (kJ mol⁻¹), bond lengths in picometres (pm) and acidities as pKₐ values. In figures, a solid wedge is a bond coming towards you out of the page, a hashed wedge is a bond going away from you behind the page, and a plain line lies in the page.
