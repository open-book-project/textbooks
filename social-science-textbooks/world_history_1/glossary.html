    Glossary¶

    The key terms of every chapter, in alphabetical order. Each entry ends with a link to the chapter where the term is defined.

    A¶

    Abrahamic faiths
    the religions of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, which all trace their origins through a common ancestor, the prophet Abraham (Chapter 11)
    Acheulean tools
    stone tools made by carefully chipping away flakes of the stone core to make them into teardrop-shaped implements that replaced the cruder Oldowan hand-axes (Chapter 2)
    animism
    the belief that a degree of spirituality exists not only in people but also in plants, inanimate objects, and natural phenomena (Chapter 2)
    annona
    the Roman government’s distribution of grain to the population, which was also a political tool for the emperor (Chapter 7)
    arquebus
    an early gun that was large and difficult to maneuver (Chapter 17)
    asceticism
    the practice of self-denial and rejection of pleasures as a way to express religious devotion (Chapter 10)
    Australopithecus
    a very distant ancestor of modern humans who lived in eastern and southern Africa between 2.5 and 4 million years ago (Chapter 2)

    B¶

    Babylonian exile
    the deportation of Judeans to Babylon after the fall of Jerusalem (Chapter 4)
    Bantu migrations
    the millennia-long expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples southward from West and Central Africa, spreading a common cultural foundation that included language, farming, and ironworking (Chapter 9)
    Bedouin
    the name given to nomadic tribes of Arabia (Chapter 10)
    Berbers
    the name used by Carthaginians, Greeks, and Romans to describe the native peoples of the Maghreb; today, this population generally self-identifies as Amazigh, or Imazighen (Chapter 9)
    Beringia
    a section of low-lying land between modern Alaska and Russia, now underwater, that once served as a land bridge between continents (Chapter 8)
    biome
    a community of vegetation and wildlife adapted to a particular climate (Chapter 9)
    Black Death
    a pandemic of the plague caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis with far-reaching economic, political, social, and cultural effects that transformed Asia, Europe, and North Africa in the fourteenth century (Chapter 16)
    Bronze Age
    the period from 3500 to 1110 BCE when bronze, an alloy of copper and tin, was the preferred material for manufacturing tools and weapons (Chapter 3)

    C¶

    cabotage
    a system wherein groups or individuals are granted the right to trade in another’s territory (Chapter 12)
    caliph
    an Islamic title designating a spiritual and secular leader (Chapter 11)
    caliphate
    an area under the control of a Muslim ruler called a caliph (Chapter 11)
    caravansary
    an inn funded by the state or wealthy individuals where travelers could spend the night and store their goods securely (Chapter 12)
    cataract
    a place in a river where the otherwise placid flow is upset by a waterfall, a shallow portion, or the presence of boulders (Chapter 9)
    centuriation
    the Roman practice of dividing land into a grid in preparation for development and agriculture (Chapter 7)
    Chan Buddhism
    a form of Mahayana Buddhism that emphasizes meditation; it is widely practiced in Japan where it is known as Zen Buddhism (Chapter 12)
    chattel slavery
    a form of slavery in which one person is owned by another as a piece of property (Chapter 4)
    chivalry
    a code of ideal conduct meant to validate the practices of noble warriors by Christianizing knightly violence and behavior (Chapter 13)
    chronological approach
    an approach to history that follows a timeline from ancient to modern (Chapter 1)
    city-state
    an independent political entity consisting of a city and surrounding territory that it controls (Chapter 3)
    clan
    a small group of several families that shared an encampment and herded or hunted together and formed the basic social unit of the seminomadic peoples of Eurasia (Chapter 5)
    clients
    less well-off Romans who relied on a patron’s gifts for subsistence (Chapter 7)
    Clovis culture
    a culture consisting of mobile bands of hunter-gatherers who camped at resource-rich locations in modest populations across North America (Chapter 8)
    Cluniac reform
    a movement that aimed to limit the influence of aristocrats in church matters (Chapter 13)
    Code of Hammurabi
    a list of judicial decisions that the Babylonian king Hammurabi had inscribed on stone pillars throughout his kingdom (Chapter 3)
    Code of Justinian
    a legal project carried out by Emperor Justinian to compile and edit Roman edicts issued from the second to the sixth century CE (Chapter 10)
    Colosseum
    a large structure in Rome that was the site of gladiatorial matches and other entertainments (Chapter 7)
    Confucianism
    a Chinese school of philosophical thought shaping morality, governance, education, and family life (Chapter 5)
    Constitutions of Melfi
    the oldest surviving written constitution in the world, which increased the power of the monarch of the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies by replacing vassals and church officials with royal bureaucrats (Chapter 14)
    culture
    all the ways in which members of a human society interact with one another and with their environment and pass these ways from generation to generation (Chapter 3)
    cuneiform
    a phonetic writing system based on the sounds of words and invented by the Sumerians in about 3000 BCE (Chapter 3)

    D¶

    Daoism
    a Chinese religion that emphasized veneration of nature, the cosmos, and mysticism (Chapter 5)
    deffufa
    a type of monumental mud-brick structure unique to Nubian civilization and believed to have served a religious function (Chapter 9)
    devshirme
    the system of acquiring Christian boys from the Balkans to be enslaved, converted to Islam, and trained to serve the Ottoman sultan (Chapter 17)
    dharma
    the Hindu concept of the right way of living as defined by cosmic law (Chapter 12)
    dictator
    a Roman Republican office with absolute authority over the state for a limited time during emergencies (Chapter 6)
    domus
    the name for a typical Roman house, as well as the family unit (Chapter 7)
    Donatist controversy
    the Christian schism in North Africa springing from the belief that church leaders who had renounced their faith to avoid persecution held no authority to perform sacraments (Chapter 15)
    drystone
    a construction method that uses interlocking stones rather than mortar (Chapter 15)
    dynatoi
    members of the Byzantine elite who often compromised imperial authority (Chapter 13)

    E¶

    ecumenical councils
    meetings organized by emperors that gathered Christian bishops from around the empire to settle matters of doctrine within the Church (Chapter 10)
    Exodus
    the mass migration of Hebrews out of Egypt under the leadership of Moses (Chapter 4)

    F¶

    Fertile Crescent
    a crescent-shaped geographical area in the Middle East where agriculture first flourished (Chapter 2)
    feudalism
    a collection of practices that bound lesser lords to greater lords through land and privileges given in return for personal and military support (Chapter 13)
    flagellants
    penitents who ritually flogged themselves in response to the Black Death as a means of appeasing God and mitigating the spread of the disease (Chapter 16)
    foederati
    foreign states and tribes that were given semiautonomy as Roman allies in exchange for pledging military service to the Roman Empire (Chapter 10)
    Forbidden City
    a walled complex of palaces, temples, and gardens built by the Ming dynasty emperors in the center of Beijing (Chapter 17)

    G¶

    genus
    a taxonomic rank that includes several similar and related species (Chapter 2)
    gladiator
    an enslaved professional fighter paid to battle before an audience, sometimes to the death (Chapter 7)
    global citizen
    a person who sees themselves as responsible to a world community rather than only a national one (Chapter 1)
    globalization
    the interconnectedness of societies and economies throughout the world as a result of trade, technology, and the adoption and sharing of various aspects of culture (Chapter 16)
    Golden Bull
    a document issued by the Holy Roman emperor Charles IV in 1356 that recognized the role of seven princes in electing Holy Roman emperors (Chapter 16)
    Golden Horde
    the Mongol group that ruled over portions of central Asia, the former lands of the Rus, and northwest Asia (Chapter 14)
    great man theory
    the view that it is enough to study the deeds and impact of important leaders to paint an accurate picture of the past (Chapter 1)
    Great Schism of 1054
    the conflict that solidified the separation of the eastern and western Christian churches (Chapter 13)
    Great Western Schism
    the period from 1378 to 1417 during which three men simultaneously served as pope of the Roman Catholic Church in western Europe (Chapter 16)

    H¶

    hadith
    the words and actions of the Islamic prophet Muhammad and his immediate successors that, along with the Quran, form the fundamental basis for Islamic law (Chapter 11)
    hajj
    the Islamic pilgrimage to Mecca (Chapter 11)
    Harappan
    a term describing the ancient Indus valley civilization, named for one of its largest cities and the first to be discovered by archaeologists (Chapter 3)
    harem
    the private household of the Ottoman sultan (Chapter 17)
    Hebrew Bible
    the holy book that, according to Jewish tradition, tells the history of the Hebrew people (Chapter 4)
    Hellenism
    a high regard for the cultural institutions of Greece including its religion, philosophy, and system of education (Chapter 7)
    Hellenistic
    a description of Greek history, language, and culture in the period 323–31 BCE (Chapter 6)
    hieratic
    a simplified form of hieroglyphics employed by Egyptian scribes for recording everyday documents such as receipts and contracts (Chapter 3)
    hieroglyphics
    a complex writing system developed around 3000 BCE in which written symbols represented both sounds and ideas (Chapter 3)
    hijra
    an Arabic term meaning “emigration” that describes a defining moment for early Muslims as they fled Mecca for Medina in 622 CE (Chapter 11)
    historical climatology
    the study of historical temperature and climate changes and their effects on human society (Chapter 16)
    historical empathy
    the ability to see the past on its own terms, without judgment or the imposition of our own modern-day attitudes (Chapter 1)
    historiography
    the study of how historians have already interpreted the past (Chapter 1)
    Homo erectus
    a member of the genus Homo who emerged in East Africa around two million years ago, living entirely on the ground and walking exclusively in an upright position (Chapter 2)
    Homo habilis
    the earliest member of the genus Homo, appearing in the archaeological record about two to three million years ago (Chapter 2)
    Homo sapiens
    modern humans, members of the genus Homo who emerged in Africa first and later migrated to other areas (Chapter 2)
    Hopewell tradition
    a widely dispersed mound-building tradition that emerged in the Eastern Woodlands around 200 BCE, linked by a network of trade routes (Chapter 8)
    humanism
    a movement born in fourteenth-century Italy that focused on the study of human beings, human nature, and human achievements rather than the study of God (Chapter 17)
    hunter-gatherers
    people who survive by employing the strategies of hunting animals and gathering wild plants rather than by planting crops and raising livestock (Chapter 2)

    I¶

    iconography
    the use of images and symbols in art (Chapter 1)
    imam
    the religious leader of Shia Muslims (Chapter 11)
    imperial cult
    the religious cult that venerated the Roman emperors as gods (Chapter 7)
    Inner Asian Steppe
    the eastern half of the Eurasian Steppe that stretches into Mongolia and runs along the northern border of China (Chapter 5)
    Inquisition
    a centuries-long effort by the church to impose religious homogeneity on Western Europe through torture and execution, if necessary (Chapter 14)
    intellectual history
    the history of ideas, which looks at the philosophies that drive people to make certain choices (Chapter 1)
    Iron Age
    the period beginning around 1200 BCE when iron became the preferred material for manufacturing tools and weapons (Chapter 4)
    Islamization
    the religious and cultural conversion of those living under Islamic rule (Chapter 11)

    J¶

    Janissaries
    the elite enslaved infantry corps of the Ottoman army (Chapter 17)
    Jewish diaspora
    the dispersion of the Jewish people beyond their ancestral homeland of Israel/Palestine following the Romans’ destruction of the Second Temple (Chapter 10)
    jihad
    a religiously infused conflict waged on behalf of Islam, or any struggle a Muslim undertakes in the name of Allah (Chapter 13)

    K¶

    karma
    a Hindu concept emphasizing the influence of good deeds and moral behavior on a person’s status in life and rebirth after death (Chapter 5)
    kentake
    a Kushite title that roughly translates to “queen mother” and was a powerful position in Meroitic Kush (Chapter 9)
    khan
    a title claimed by warrior-kings to unite various tribes into powerful confederations and empires (Chapter 5)
    kurultai
    a proto-democratic gathering of a Mongol leader’s followers, called to reach agreement on major political decisions (Chapter 14)

    L¶

    lanista
    the trainer and manager of a group of gladiators (Chapter 7)
    Late Antiquity
    a transitional period between the ancient and medieval worlds that occurred from roughly 150 to 750 CE (Chapter 10)
    latifundia
    large agricultural estates in the countryside, worked by enslaved people to produce profit for the owner (Chapter 7)
    Legalism
    a school of philosophical thought that helped dynasties such as the Qin use uniform laws and codes to reform and strengthen rulers (Chapter 5)
    legion
    the basic unit of the Roman army, made up of around five thousand soldiers (Chapter 7)
    Levant
    a historical geographical term referring to an area in the eastern Mediterranean consisting roughly of modern Israel, Jordan, Lebanon, Palestine, and Syria (Chapter 6)
    Linear A
    a script developed by the Minoans but not yet deciphered by modern scholars (Chapter 6)
    Linear B
    a Mycenaean script developed from Linear A that was used to write an early form of the Greek language (Chapter 6)
    Little Ice Age
    a period in the early fourteenth century during which global mean temperatures dropped an average of 0.6°C, resulting in droughts and decreased agricultural productivity (Chapter 16)
    lugal
    the Sumerians’ term for their ruler (Chapter 3)

    M¶

    Maghreb
    the western half of North Africa, including most of present-day Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya (Chapter 9)
    Magna Carta
    an English document that reiterated existing rights of vassals, confirmed the papal position that the church is above the state, and spelled out some basic rights of commoners (Chapter 14)
    Mamluk Sultanate
    a state in Egypt and the Levant administered and defended by educated, formerly enslaved men called mamluks (Chapter 17)
    mamluks
    educated, formerly enslaved men who served as soldiers and administrators in Islamic societies beginning in the ninth century (Chapter 13)
    mandarins
    top officials in the Imperial Chinese bureaucracy, selected by a series of exams on Confucian texts (Chapter 14)
    manorialism
    a medieval economic system of agricultural production directed by a lord and carried out by serfs or other varieties of unfree laborers (Chapter 13)
    manumission
    the process of releasing a person from slavery, usually in front of a magistrate or via a slaveholder’s will, or through a person’s purchasing their own freedom (Chapter 7)
    mawali
    non-Arab converts to Islam in the early Islamic period who had to be adopted by an Arab tribe as part of the conversion process (Chapter 11)
    Medieval Warm Period
    a time of more temperate climate across the globe from the tenth through the thirteenth century (Chapter 16)
    Mississippian tradition
    a widely dispersed mound-building tradition that created a number of urban settlements linked by trading networks in the southeastern United States around 700 (Chapter 8)
    moksha
    the release from samsara and the karmic cycle together with the attainment of a complete understanding of the world (Chapter 12)
    monsoon
    the seasonal pattern of wind and rainfall across South Asia (Chapter 3)
    Mousterian tools
    stone tools and hand-axes made beginning around 250,000 years ago and consisting of flakes rather than cores (Chapter 2)
    mystery religions
    religious cults that featured secret rituals (the so-called mysteries) and became popular in Hellenistic cities (Chapter 6)
    “Mandate of Heaven”
    the favor of the gods that conferred a right to rule but could be lost by those less worthy (Chapter 5)

    N¶

    Nazca Lines
    a group of geoglyphs made on the desert floor in southern Peru representing both geometric patterns and images of animals (Chapter 8)
    Neanderthals
    members of the genus Homo who evolved from Homo erectus and lived in Europe and western Asia between 30,000 and 200,000 years ago (Chapter 2)
    Neolithic Age
    the final phase of the Paleolithic Age, beginning around twelve thousand years ago when human populations began growing crops and domesticating animals (Chapter 2)
    Neolithic Revolution
    the shift from hunting and gathering to a life based primarily on agriculture (Chapter 2)
    nomarchs
    regional governors in ancient Egypt (Chapter 3)
    Norte Chico
    the culture of partially settled agricultural communities in the Andes region; also known as the Caral civilization (Chapter 8)
    Nubia
    the name Egyptians gave to the expansive area south of the first cataract and extending into sub-Saharan Africa; it included the Kingdom of Kush (Chapter 9)

    O¶

    Oldowan tools
    sharpened stones used until about 1.7 million years ago for a variety of cutting, scraping, and chopping purposes (Chapter 2)
    optimates
    politicians who supported the old order and the traditional leadership of elites (Chapter 6)
    Outremer
    the French name for the four Crusader States created after the First Crusade, the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch, the County of Tripoli, and the Kingdom of Jerusalem (Chapter 13)

    P¶

    Paleolithic Age
    the period of time beginning as early as 3.3 million years ago until nearly twelve thousand years ago, when our distant pre-human ancestors began using stone tools for a variety of purposes (Chapter 2)
    papacy
    the set of administrative structures associated with the government of the Catholic Church primarily—but not exclusively—linked with the city of Rome (Chapter 13)
    papyrus
    a type of paper Egyptians made from a common reed plant growing along the Nile (Chapter 3)
    pastoralists
    nomadic people who rely on herds of domesticated animals for subsistence (Chapter 3)
    paterfamilias
    the patriarch of an extended Roman family, with authority over his wife, children, and any other dependents (Chapter 7)
    patron
    a wealthy Roman aristocrat sought by clients in need of assistance (Chapter 7)
    Peking Man
    a subspecies of Homo erectus identified by fossil remains found in northern China (Chapter 5)
    People of the Felt Walls
    Temujin’s name for his ethnically and linguistically diverse followers (Chapter 14)
    pharaoh
    the title of the Egyptian ruler, translated as “big house” (Chapter 3)
    polis
    a city-state in Ancient Greece (Chapter 6)
    polytheists
    people who worship multiple gods, usually associated with different aspects of the natural world (Chapter 3)
    populares
    politicians who sought the political support of discontented groups in Roman society (Chapter 6)
    primary cause
    the most immediate reason an event occurred (Chapter 1)
    primary source
    a document, object, or other source material from the time period under study (Chapter 1)
    principate
    the political system established by Augustus Caesar after 27 BCE, which relied on Rome’s traditional institutions and practices to legitimize a military dictatorship (Chapter 6)
    progressive history
    a school of thought that views history as a straight line to a specific and more democratic destination (Chapter 1)
    proletariat
    the landless working class (Chapter 6)
    publicani
    provincial contractors who bid for the right to collect taxes and profited from the excess money they gathered (Chapter 7)
    Pueblo architecture
    a building style of the early American Southwest that relied on stone or wooden frames covered in adobe clay (Chapter 8)
    Pure Land Buddhism
    a branch of Mahayana Buddhism holding that all believers can be reborn into a place of salvation, the Pure Land (Chapter 12)

    Q¶

    Quran
    the holy scripture of Islam, which Muslims believe was given to humanity by God through Muhammad (Chapter 11)

    R¶

    Ramesside kings
    the line of kings that ruled New Kingdom Egypt following the reign of Ramses I (Chapter 4)
    Rashidun
    a term meaning “rightly guided” that describes the first four caliphs after Muhammad’s death: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali (Chapter 11)
    Red Turbans
    a secret peasant society that rose up against Mongol rule during the Yuan dynasty (Chapter 17)
    Renaissance
    a period of intellectual and artistic rebirth inspired by the cultural achievements of ancient Greece and Rome (Chapter 17)
    revisionism
    the process of altering our interpretation of historical events by adding new elements and perspectives (Chapter 1)
    rhetoric
    the way words are used and put together in speaking or writing (Chapter 1)
    Ring of Fire
    the boundary line of a zone of intense seismic activity in the Pacific Ocean (Chapter 5)

    S¶

    Sahel
    an east–west belt of semiarid grassland that forms a transitional zone between the Sahara to the north and the equatorial rainforest to the south (Chapter 9)
    samsara
    a Hindu concept explaining the continuance of the soul after death and its transformation (Chapter 5)
    samurai
    a member of the elite warrior class of Japan, governed by a strict code of behavior (Chapter 12)
    satrapy
    one of twenty governing districts in Persia administered by royal governors called satraps, who answered directly to the king (Chapter 4)
    savanna
    a grassy plain with scattered trees found to the north and south of the tropical African rainforest (Chapter 9)
    secondary source
    a document, object, or other source material written or created after the time period under study (Chapter 1)
    serfs
    unfree peasants who owed labor to a feudal lord and lived under the lord’s authority (Chapter 13)
    sharia
    Islamic religious law (Chapter 14)
    Shia
    one of the two umbrella sects of Islam, whose members believe leadership of the Muslim community should reside in the family of Muhammad only through his son-in-law Ali (Chapter 11)
    shogun
    a Japanese military commander-in-chief (Chapter 12)
    Silk Roads
    a series of trade routes circulating luxury goods to and from China and parts of central Asia, India, and the Middle East (Chapter 5)
    social constructs
    ideas such as class and gender created and accepted by the people in a society that influence the way they think and behave (Chapter 1)
    social history
    a field of history that looks at all classes and categories of people, not just elites (Chapter 1)
    social stratification
    the hierarchical order of society in which people sharing the same level of wealth and status make up a distinct class or strata (Chapter 3)
    specialization
    a societal characteristic in which people perform specific tasks, such as farming (farmer) or producing tools and clothing (artisan), that contribute to the well-being of the community (Chapter 3)
    Struggle of the Orders
    a political contest during the first centuries of the Republic in which Rome’s commoners sought equal rights with elites (Chapter 6)
    Sufism
    the mystical expression of Islamic faith (Chapter 13)
    sultan
    a ruler who claims authority over the Islamic community but not necessarily the title of caliph (Chapter 13)
    Sunni
    the larger of the two umbrella sects of Islam, whose adherents did not require leadership of the community to come specifically from the descendants of Muhammad through Ali (Chapter 11)

    T¶

    Terracotta Army
    a collection of life-size clay statues of soldiers, officials, servants, and horses of the Qin emperor and buried in his tomb near Xi’an (Chapter 5)
    tetrarchy
    the rule of four emperors, two senior and two junior, established by the emperor Diocletian to quell the Crisis of the Third Century (Chapter 10)
    Theodosian Code
    a document initiated by the emperor Theodosius II compiling laws from around the empire that had been issued since the early fourth century (Chapter 10)
    Three Han
    the three groups (the Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan) in southern Korea that were among the earliest with tribal chieftains (Chapter 5)
    timar
    a right granted to subjects of the Ottoman sultan to collect taxes in a given area (Chapter 17)
    Triple Alliance
    an alliance formed in 1428 between the Aztecs and two neighboring city-states, Texcoco and Tlacopan (Chapter 8)
    Twelve Tables
    the first set of written laws in Rome, from about 450 BCE (Chapter 6)

    U¶

    ulama
    a class of religious clerics and scholars who act as the primary interpreters of Islamic law (Chapter 11)
    ummah
    the community of Muslims (Chapter 11)

    V¶

    vassal state
    a state or kingdom that is nominally independent in the running of its internal affairs but must submit to the demands of a dominating empire and usually provide tribute to it (Chapter 4)
    vestal virgins
    the priesthood of six women who took a vow of chastity and maintained the sacred fire in Rome (Chapter 7)

    Y¶

    yam
    horse relay stations established by Chinggis Khan for long-distance communication on military campaigns; they were later expanded into rest areas and supply depots (Chapter 14)
    yassa
    Chinggis Khan’s law code, designed to eliminate the sources of conflict in steppe society and bring harmony to his people (Chapter 14)

    Z¶

    ziggurat
    an immense stepped tower with a flat top built of mud-brick that served as a temple in Sumerian cities (Chapter 3)
    Zoroastrianism
    the religion of the ancient Persians, named for its founder Zarathustra, pronounced Zoroaster in Greek (Chapter 4)
