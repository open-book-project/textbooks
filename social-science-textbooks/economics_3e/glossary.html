    Glossary¶

    The key terms of every chapter, in alphabetical order. Each entry ends with a link to the chapter where the term is defined.

    A¶

    absolute advantage
    when one country has more resources, more productive resources, or a natural endowment to produce a good compared to another country; when a country can produce more of a good compared to another country (Chapter 33)
    accounting profit
    total revenues minus explicit costs, including depreciation (Chapter 7)
    acquisition
    when one firm purchases another (Chapter 11)
    actual rate of return
    the total rate of return, including capital gains and interest paid on an investment at the end of a time period (Chapter 17)
    adaptive expectations
    the theory that people look at past experience and gradually adapt their beliefs and behavior as circumstances change (Chapter 26)
    additional external cost
    additional costs incurred by third parties outside the production process when a unit of output is produced (Chapter 12)
    adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)
    a loan a borrower uses to purchase a home in which the interest rate varies with market interest rates (Chapter 22)
    adverse selection
    when groups with inherently higher risks than the average person seek out insurance, thus straining the insurance system (Chapter 16)
    adverse selection of wage cuts argument
    if employers reduce wages for all workers, the best will leave (Chapter 21)
    affirmative action
    active efforts by government or businesses that give special rights to minorities in hiring, promotion, or access to education to make up for past discrimination (Chapter 14)
    aggregate demand (AD)
    the amount of total spending on domestic goods and services in an economy (Chapter 24)
    aggregate demand (AD) curve
    the total spending on domestic goods and services at each price level (Chapter 24)
    aggregate demand/aggregate supply model
    a model that shows what determines total supply or total demand for the economy, and how total demand and total supply interact at the macroeconomic level (Chapter 24)
    aggregate production function
    the process whereby an economy as a whole turns economic inputs such as human capital, physical capital, and technology into output measured as GDP per capita (Chapter 20)
    aggregate supply (AS)
    the total quantity of output (i.e. real GDP) firms will produce and sell (Chapter 24)
    aggregate supply (AS) curve
    the total quantity of output (i.e. real GDP) that firms will produce and sell at each price level (Chapter 24)
    allocative efficiency
    when the mix of goods produced represents the mix that society most desires (Chapter 2)
    ample reserves
    when banks are holding an amount of reserves significantly above the minimum required amount; generally a choice made by banks during and after the Great Recession (Chapter 27)
    anti-dumping laws
    laws that block imports sold below the cost of production and impose tariffs that would increase the price of these imports to reflect their cost of production (Chapter 34)
    antitrust laws
    laws that give government the power to block certain mergers, and even in some cases to break up large firms into smaller ones (Chapter 11)
    appreciating
    when a currency is worth more in terms of other currencies; also called “strengthening” (Chapter 29)
    arbitrage
    the process of buying a good and selling goods across borders to take advantage of international price differences (Chapter 29)
    asset
    item of value that a firm or an individual owns (Chapter 27)
    asset–liability time mismatch
    customers can withdraw a bank’s liabilities in the short term while customers repay its assets in the long term (Chapter 27)
    asymmetric information
    a situation where the seller or the buyer has more information than the other regarding the quality of the item for sale (Chapter 16)
    automatic stabilizers
    tax and spending rules that have the effect of slowing down the rate of decrease in aggregate demand when the economy slows down and restraining aggregate demand when the economy speeds up, without any additional change in legislation (Chapter 30)
    average profit
    profit divided by the quantity of output produced; also known as profit margin (Chapter 7)
    average total cost
    total cost divided by the quantity of output (Chapter 7)
    average variable cost
    variable cost divided by the quantity of output (Chapter 7)

    B¶

    balance of trade (trade balance)
    the gap, if any, between a nation’s exports and imports (Chapter 23)
    balance sheet
    an accounting tool that lists assets and liabilities (Chapter 27)
    balanced budget
    when government spending and taxes are equal (Chapter 30)
    bank capital
    a bank’s net worth (Chapter 27)
    bank run
    when depositors race to the bank to withdraw their deposits for fear that otherwise they would be lost (Chapter 28)
    barriers to entry
    the legal, technological, or market forces that may discourage or prevent potential competitors from entering a market (Chapter 9)
    barter
    literally, trading one good or service for another, without using money (Chapter 27)
    base year
    arbitrary year whose value as an index number economists define as 100; inflation from the base year to other years can easily be seen by comparing the index number in the other year to the index number in the base year—for example, 100; so, if the index number for a year is 105, then there has been exactly 5% inflation between that year and the base year (Chapter 22)
    basic quantity equation of money
    money supply × velocity = nominal GDP (Chapter 28)
    basket of goods and services
    a hypothetical group of different items, with specified quantities of each one meant to represent a “typical” set of consumer purchases, used as a basis for calculating how the price level changes over time (Chapter 22)
    behavioral economics
    a branch of economics that seeks to enrich the understanding of decision-making by integrating the insights of psychology and by investigating how given dollar amounts can mean different things to individuals depending on the situation (Chapter 6)
    bilateral monopoly
    a labor market with a monopsony on the demand side and a union on the supply side (Chapter 14)
    biodiversity
    the full spectrum of animal and plant genetic material (Chapter 12)
    bond
    a financial contract through which a borrower like a corporation, a city or state, or the federal government agrees to repay the amount that it borrowed and also a rate of interest over a period of time in the future (Chapter 17)
    bond yield
    the rate of return a bond is expected to pay at the time of purchase (Chapter 17)
    bondholder
    someone who owns bonds and receives the interest payments (Chapter 17)
    break even point
    level of output where the marginal cost curve intersects the average cost curve at the minimum point of AC; if the price is at this point, the firm is earning zero economic profits (Chapter 8)
    budget constraint
    all possible consumption combinations of goods that someone can afford, given the prices of goods, when all income is spent; the boundary of the opportunity set (Chapter 2)
    budget constraint (or budget line)
    shows the possible combinations of two goods that are affordable given a consumer’s limited income (Chapter 6)
    budget deficit
    when the federal government spends more money than it receives in taxes in a given year (Chapter 30)
    budget surplus
    when the government receives more money in taxes than it spends in a year (Chapter 30)
    bundling
    a situation in which multiple products are sold as one (Chapter 11)
    business cycle
    the economy's relatively short-term movement in and out of recession (Chapter 19)

    C¶

    capital deepening
    an increase by society in the average level of physical and/or human capital per person (Chapter 20)
    capital gain
    a financial gain from buying an asset, like a share of stock or a house, and later selling it at a higher price (Chapter 17)
    cartel
    a group of firms that collude to produce the monopoly output and sell at the monopoly price (Chapter 10)
    central bank
    institution which conducts a nation’s monetary policy and regulates its banking system (Chapter 28)
    certificate of deposit (CD)
    a mechanism for a saver to deposit funds at a bank and promise to leave them at the bank for a time, in exchange for a higher interest rate (Chapter 17)
    ceteris paribus
    other things being equal (Chapter 3)
    checking account
    a bank account that typically pays little or no interest, but that gives easy access to money, either by writing a check or by using a “debit card” (Chapter 17)
    circular flow diagram
    a diagram that views the economy as consisting of households and firms interacting in a goods and services market and a labor market (Chapter 1)
    coins and currency in circulation
    the coins and bills that circulate in an economy that are not held by the U.S Treasury, at the Federal Reserve Bank, or in bank vaults (Chapter 27)
    coinsurance
    when an insurance policyholder pays a percentage of a loss, and the insurance company pays the remaining cost (Chapter 16)
    collateral
    something valuable—often property or equipment—that a lender would have a right to seize and sell if the buyer does not repay the loan (Chapter 16)
    collective bargaining
    negotiations between unions and a firm or firms (Chapter 14)
    collusion
    when firms act together to reduce output and keep prices high (Chapter 10)
    command economy
    an economy where economic decisions are passed down from government authority and where the government owns the resources (Chapter 1)
    command-and-control regulation
    laws that specify allowable quantities of pollution and that also may detail which pollution-control technologies one must use (Chapter 12)
    commodity money
    an item that is used as money, but which also has value from its use as something other than money (Chapter 27)
    commodity-backed currencies
    dollar bills or other currencies with values backed up by gold or another commodity (Chapter 27)
    common market
    economic agreement between countries to allow free trade in goods, services, labor, and financial capital between members while having a common external trade policy (Chapter 34)
    comparative advantage
    when a country can produce a good at a lower cost in terms of other goods; or, when a country has a lower opportunity cost of production (Chapter 2)
    complements
    goods that are often used together so that consumption of one good tends to enhance consumption of the other (Chapter 3)
    compound growth rate
    the rate of growth when multiplied by a base that includes past GDP growth (Chapter 20)
    compound interest
    an interest rate calculation on the principal plus the accumulated interest (Chapter 17)
    concentration ratio
    an early tool to measure the degree of monopoly power in an industry; measures what share of the total sales in the industry are accounted for by the largest firms, typically the top four to eight firms (Chapter 11)
    constant returns to scale
    expanding all inputs proportionately does not change the average cost of production (Chapter 7)
    constant unitary elasticity
    when a given percent price change in price leads to an equal percentage change in quantity demanded or supplied (Chapter 5)
    consumer equilibrium
    point on the budget line where the consumer gets the most satisfaction; this occurs when the ratio of the prices of goods is equal to the ratio of the marginal utilities. (Chapter 6)
    Consumer Price Index (CPI)
    a measure of inflation that U.S. government statisticians calculate based on the price level from a fixed basket of goods and services that represents the average consumer's purchases (Chapter 22)
    consumer surplus
    the extra benefit consumers receive from buying a good or service, measured by what the individuals would have been willing to pay minus the amount that they actually paid (Chapter 3)
    contractionary fiscal policy
    tax increases or cuts in government spending designed to decrease aggregate demand and reduce inflationary pressures (Chapter 25)
    contractionary fiscal policy
    fiscal policy that decreases the level of aggregate demand, either through cuts in government spending or increases in taxes (Chapter 30)
    contractionary monetary policy
    a monetary policy that reduces the supply of money and loans (Chapter 28)
    contractual rights
    the rights of individuals to enter into agreements with others regarding the use of their property providing recourse through the legal system in the event of noncompliance (Chapter 20)
    convergence
    pattern in which economies with low per capita incomes grow faster than economies with high per capita incomes (Chapter 20)
    converging economy
    economy of a country that has demonstrated the ability to catch up to the technology leaders by investing in both physical and human capital (Chapter 32)
    coordination argument
    downward wage and price flexibility requires perfect information about the level of lower compensation acceptable to other laborers and market participants (Chapter 25)
    copayment
    when an insurance policyholder must pay a small amount for each service, before insurance covers the rest (Chapter 16)
    copyright
    a form of legal protection to prevent copying, for commercial purposes, original works of authorship, including books and music (Chapter 9)
    core inflation index
    a measure of inflation typically calculated by taking the CPI and excluding volatile economic variables such as food and energy prices to better measure the underlying and persistent trend in long-term prices (Chapter 22)
    corporate bond
    a bond issued by firms that wish to borrow (Chapter 17)
    corporate governance
    the name economists give to the institutions that are supposed to watch over top executives in companies that shareholders own (Chapter 17)
    corporate income tax
    a tax imposed on corporate profits (Chapter 30)
    corporation
    a business owned by shareholders who have limited liability for the company’s debt yet a share of the company’s profits; may be private or public and may or may not have publicly-traded stock (Chapter 17)
    cosigner
    another person or firm who legally pledges to repay some or all of the money on a loan if the original borrower does not (Chapter 16)
    cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs)
    a contractual provision that wage increases will keep up with inflation (Chapter 22)
    cost-plus regulation
    when regulators permit a regulated firm to cover its costs and to make a normal level of profit (Chapter 11)
    countercyclical
    moving in the opposite direction of the business cycle of economic downturns and upswings (Chapter 28)
    coupon rate
    the interest rate paid on a bond; can be annual or semi-annual (Chapter 17)
    credit card
    immediately transfers money from the credit card company’s checking account to the seller, and at the end of the month the user owes the money to the credit card company; a credit card is a short-term loan (Chapter 27)
    cross-price elasticity of demand
    the percentage change in the quantity of good A that is demanded as a result of a percentage change in the price of good B (Chapter 5)
    crowding out
    federal spending and borrowing causes interest rates to rise and business investment to fall (Chapter 30)
    current account balance
    a broad measure of the balance of trade that includes trade in goods and services, as well as international flows of income and foreign aid (Chapter 23)
    cyclical unemployment
    unemployment closely tied to the business cycle, like higher unemployment during a recession (Chapter 21)

    D¶

    deadweight loss
    the loss in social surplus that occurs when a market produces an inefficient quantity (Chapter 3)
    debit card
    a card that lets the person make purchases, and the financial institution immediately deducts cost from that person’s checking account (Chapter 17)
    debit card
    like a check, is an instruction to the user’s bank to transfer money directly and immediately from your bank account to the seller (Chapter 27)
    deductible
    an amount that the insurance policyholders must pay out of their own pocket before the insurance coverage pays anything (Chapter 16)
    deflation
    negative inflation; most prices in the economy are falling (Chapter 22)
    demand
    the relationship between price and the quantity demanded of a certain good or service (Chapter 3)
    demand curve
    a graphic representation of the relationship between price and quantity demanded of a certain good or service, with quantity on the horizontal axis and the price on the vertical axis (Chapter 3)
    demand deposit
    checkable deposit in banks that is available by making a cash withdrawal or writing a check (Chapter 27)
    demand schedule
    a table that shows a range of prices for a certain good or service and the quantity demanded at each price (Chapter 3)
    deposit insurance
    an insurance system that makes sure depositors in a bank do not lose their money, even if the bank goes bankrupt (Chapter 28)
    depository institution
    institution that accepts money deposits and then uses these to make loans (Chapter 27)
    depreciating
    when a currency is worth less in terms of other currencies; also called “weakening” (Chapter 29)
    depreciation
    the process by which capital ages over time and therefore loses its value (Chapter 19)
    depression
    an especially lengthy and deep decline in output (Chapter 19)
    deregulation
    removing government controls over setting prices and quantities in certain industries (Chapter 9)
    differentiated product
    a product that consumers perceive as distinctive in some way (Chapter 10)
    diminishing marginal productivity
    general rule that as a firm employs more labor, eventually the amount of additional output produced declines (Chapter 7)
    diminishing marginal utility
    the common pattern that each marginal unit of a good consumed provides less of an addition to utility than the previous unit (Chapter 6)
    discount rate
    the interest rate charged by the central bank on the loans that it gives to other commercial banks (Chapter 28)
    discouraged workers
    those who have stopped looking for employment due to the lack of suitable positions available (Chapter 21)
    discretionary fiscal policy
    the government passes a new law that explicitly changes overall tax or spending levels with the intent of influencing the level of overall economic activity (Chapter 30)
    discrimination
    actions based on the belief that members of a certain group or groups are in some way inferior solely because of a factor such as race, gender, or religion (Chapter 14)
    diseconomies of scale
    the long-run average cost of producing output increases as total output increases (Chapter 7)
    disposable income
    income after taxes (Chapter 25)
    disruptive market change
    innovative new product or production technology which disrupts the status quo in a market, leading the innovators to earn more income and profits and the other firms to lose income and profits, unless they can come up with their own innovations (Chapter 34)
    diversification
    investing in a wide range of companies to reduce the level of risk (Chapter 17)
    diversify
    making loans or investments with a variety of firms, to reduce the risk of being adversely affected by events at one or a few firms (Chapter 27)
    dividend
    a direct payment from a firm to its shareholders (Chapter 17)
    division of labor
    the way in which different workers divide required tasks to produce a good or service (Chapter 1)
    dollarize
    a country that is not the United States uses the U.S. dollar as its currency (Chapter 29)
    double coincidence of wants
    a situation in which two people each want some good or service that the other person can provide (Chapter 27)
    double counting
    a potential mistake to avoid in measuring GDP, in which output is counted more than once as it travels through the stages of production (Chapter 19)
    dumping
    selling internationally traded goods below their cost of production (Chapter 34)
    duopoly
    an oligopoly with only two firms (Chapter 10)
    durable good
    long-lasting good like a car or a refrigerator (Chapter 19)

    E¶

    earned income tax credit (EITC)
    a method of assisting the working poor through the tax system (Chapter 15)
    East Asian Tigers
    the economies of Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong, and South Korea, which maintained high growth rates and rapid export-led industrialization between the early 1960s and 1990 allowing them to converge with the technological leaders in high-income countries (Chapter 32)
    economic profit
    total revenues minus total costs (explicit plus implicit costs) (Chapter 7)
    economic surplus
    see social surplus (Chapter 3)
    economic union
    economic agreement between countries to allow free trade between members, a common external trade policy, and coordinated monetary and fiscal policies (Chapter 34)
    economics
    the study of how humans make choices under conditions of scarcity (Chapter 1)
    economies of scale
    when the average cost of producing each individual unit declines as total output increases (Chapter 1)
    economies of scale
    the long-run average cost of producing output decreases as total output increases (Chapter 7)
    effective income tax
    percentage of total taxes paid divided by total income (Chapter 15)
    efficiency wage theory
    the theory that the productivity of workers, either individually or as a group, will increase if the employer pays them more (Chapter 21)
    elastic demand
    when the elasticity of demand is greater than one, indicating a high responsiveness of quantity demanded or supplied to changes in price (Chapter 5)
    elastic supply
    when the elasticity of either supply is greater than one, indicating a high responsiveness of quantity demanded or supplied to changes in price (Chapter 5)
    elasticity
    an economics concept that measures responsiveness of one variable to changes in another variable (Chapter 5)
    elasticity of savings
    the percentage change in the quantity of savings divided by the percentage change in interest rates (Chapter 5)
    Employment Cost Index
    a measure of inflation based on wages paid in the labor market (Chapter 22)
    entry
    the long-run process of firms entering an industry in response to industry profits (Chapter 8)
    equilibrium
    the situation where quantity demanded is equal to the quantity supplied; the combination of price and quantity where there is no economic pressure from surpluses or shortages that would cause price or quantity to change (Chapter 3)
    equilibrium price
    the price where quantity demanded is equal to quantity supplied (Chapter 3)
    equilibrium quantity
    the quantity at which quantity demanded and quantity supplied are equal for a certain price level (Chapter 3)
    equity
    the monetary value a homeowner would have after selling the house and repaying any outstanding bank loans used to buy the house (Chapter 17)
    estate and gift tax
    a tax on people who pass assets to the next generation—either after death or during life in the form of gifts (Chapter 30)
    estate tax
    a tax imposed on the value of an inheritance (Chapter 15)
    excess demand
    at the existing price, the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied; also called a shortage (Chapter 3)
    excess reserves
    reserves banks hold that exceed the legally mandated limit (Chapter 28)
    excess supply
    at the existing price, quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded; also called a surplus (Chapter 3)
    exchange rate
    the price of one currency in terms of another currency (Chapter 19)
    excise tax
    a tax on a specific good—on gasoline, tobacco, and alcohol (Chapter 30)
    exclusive dealing
    an agreement that a dealer will sell only products from one manufacturer (Chapter 11)
    exit
    the long-run process of firms reducing production and shutting down in response to industry losses (Chapter 8)
    expansionary fiscal policy
    tax cuts or increases in government spending designed to stimulate aggregate demand and move the economy out of recession (Chapter 25)
    expansionary fiscal policy
    fiscal policy that increases the level of aggregate demand, either through increases in government spending or cuts in taxes (Chapter 30)
    expansionary monetary policy
    a monetary policy that increases the supply of money and the quantity of loans (Chapter 28)
    expected inflation
    a future rate of inflation that consumers and firms build into current decision making (Chapter 26)
    expected rate of return
    how much a project or an investment is expected to return to the investor, either in future interest payments, capital gains, or increased profitability (Chapter 17)
    expenditure multiplier
    Keynesian concept that asserts that a change in autonomous spending causes a more than proportionate change in real GDP (Chapter 25)
    explicit costs
    out-of-pocket costs for a firm, for example, payments for wages and salaries, rent, or materials (Chapter 7)
    exports
    products (goods and services) made domestically and sold abroad (Chapter 1)
    exports of goods and services as a percentage of GDP
    the dollar value of exports divided by the dollar value of a country’s GDP (Chapter 23)
    external benefits (or positive externalities)
    beneficial spillovers to a third party of parties, who did not purchase the good or service that provided the externalities (Chapter 13)
    externality
    a market exchange that affects a third party who is outside or “external” to the exchange; sometimes called a “spillover” (Chapter 12)

    F¶

    face value
    the amount that the bond issuer or borrower agrees to pay the investor (Chapter 17)
    factors of production
    the resources such as labor, materials, and machinery that are used to produce goods and services; also called inputs (Chapter 3)
    factors of production (or inputs)
    resources that firms use to produce their products, for example, labor and capital (Chapter 7)
    federal funds rate
    the interest rate at which one bank lends funds to another bank overnight (Chapter 28)
    fee-for-service
    when medical care providers are paid according to the services they provide (Chapter 16)
    fiat money
    has no intrinsic value, but is declared by a government to be the country's legal tender (Chapter 27)
    final good and service
    output used directly for consumption, investment, government, and trade purposes; contrast with “intermediate good” (Chapter 19)
    financial capital
    the international flows of money that facilitates trade and investment (Chapter 23)
    financial intermediary
    an institution, like a bank, that receives money from savers and provides funds to borrowers (Chapter 17)
    financial intermediary
    an institution that operates between a saver with financial assets to invest and an entity who will borrow those assets and pay a rate of return (Chapter 27)
    firm
    an organization that combines inputs of labor, capital, land, and raw or finished component materials to produce outputs. (Chapter 7)
    first rule of labor markets
    an employer will never pay a worker more than the value of the worker's marginal productivity to the firm (Chapter 14)
    fiscal policy
    economic policies that involve government spending and taxes (Chapter 1)
    fixed cost
    cost of the fixed inputs; expenditure that a firm must make before production starts and that does not change regardless of the production level (Chapter 7)
    fixed inputs
    factors of production that can’t be easily increased or decreased in a short period of time (Chapter 7)
    floating exchange rate
    a country lets the exchange rate market determine its currency's value (Chapter 29)
    foreign direct investment (FDI)
    purchasing more than ten percent of a firm or starting a new enterprise in another country (Chapter 29)
    foreign exchange market
    the market in which people use one currency to buy another currency (Chapter 29)
    four-firm concentration ratio
    the percentage of the total sales in the industry that are accounted for by the largest four firms (Chapter 11)
    free rider
    those who want others to pay for the public good and then plan to use the good themselves; if many people act as free riders, the public good may never be provided (Chapter 13)
    free trade agreement
    economic agreement between countries to allow free trade between members (Chapter 34)
    frictional unemployment
    unemployment that occurs as workers move between jobs (Chapter 21)
    full-employment GDP
    another name for potential GDP, when the economy is producing at its potential and unemployment is at the natural rate of unemployment (Chapter 24)
    fungible
    the idea that units of a good, such as dollars, ounces of gold, or barrels of oil are capable of mutual substitution with each other and carry equal value to the individual (Chapter 6)

    G¶

    gain from trade
    a country that can consume more than it can produce as a result of specialization and trade (Chapter 33)
    game theory
    a branch of mathematics that economists use to analyze situations in which players must make decisions and then receive payoffs based on what decisions the other players make (Chapter 10)
    GDP deflator
    a measure of inflation based on the prices of all the GDP components (Chapter 22)
    GDP per capita
    GDP divided by the population (Chapter 19)
    General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)
    forum in which nations could come together to negotiate reductions in tariffs and other barriers to trade; the precursor to the World Trade Organization (Chapter 34)
    globalization
    the trend in which buying and selling in markets have increasingly crossed national borders (Chapter 1)
    goods and services market
    a market in which firms are sellers of what they produce and households are buyers (Chapter 1)
    gross domestic product (GDP)
    measure of the size of total production in an economy (Chapter 1)
    gross domestic product (GDP)
    the value of the output of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year (Chapter 19)
    gross national product (GNP)
    includes what is produced domestically and what is produced by domestic labor and business abroad in a year (Chapter 19)
    growth consensus
    a series of studies that show, statistically, that 70% of the differences in income per person across the world is explained by differences in physical capital (savings/investment) (Chapter 32)

    H¶

    hard peg
    an exchange rate policy in which the central bank sets a fixed and unchanging value for the exchange rate (Chapter 29)
    Head Start program
    a program for early childhood education directed at families with limited educational and financial resources. (Chapter 31)
    health maintenance organization (HMO)
    an organization that provides health care and is paid a fixed amount per person enrolled in the plan—regardless of how many services are provided (Chapter 16)
    hedge
    using a financial transaction as protection against risk (Chapter 29)
    Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)
    approach to measuring market concentration by adding the square of the market share of each firm in the industry (Chapter 11)
    high-income country
    nation with a per capita income of $12,475 or more; typically has high levels of human and physical capital (Chapter 32)
    high-yield bonds
    bonds that offer relatively high interest rates to compensate for their relatively high chance of default (Chapter 17)
    human capital
    the accumulated skills and education of workers (Chapter 20)
    hyperinflation
    an outburst of high inflation that often occurs (although not exclusively) when economies shift from a controlled economy to a market-oriented economy (Chapter 22)

    I¶

    imperfect information

    unemployment that occurs because individuals lack skills valued by employers (Chapter 21)
    structure
    building used as residence, factory, office building, retail store, or for other purposes (Chapter 19)
    substitute
    a good that can replace another to some extent, so that greater consumption of one good can mean less of the other (Chapter 3)
    substitution bias
    an inflation rate calculated using a fixed basket of goods over time tends to overstate the true rise in the cost of living, because it does not take into account that the person can substitute away from goods whose prices rise considerably (Chapter 22)
    substitution effect
    when a price changes, consumers have an incentive to consume less of the good with a relatively higher price and more of the good with a relatively lower price; always happens simultaneously with an income effect (Chapter 6)
    sunk costs
    costs that we make in the past that we cannot recover (Chapter 2)
    Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP)
    a federally funded program, started in 1964, in which each month poor people receive SNAP cards they can use to buy food (Chapter 15)
    supply
    the relationship between price and the quantity supplied of a certain good or service (Chapter 3)
    supply curve
    a line that shows the relationship between price and quantity supplied on a graph, with quantity supplied on the horizontal axis and price on the vertical axis (Chapter 3)
    supply schedule
    a table that shows a range of prices for a good or service and the quantity supplied at each price (Chapter 3)
    surplus
    at the existing price, quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded; also called excess supply (Chapter 3)

    T¶

    T-account
    a balance sheet with a two-column format, with the T-shape formed by the vertical line down the middle and the horizontal line under the column headings for “Assets” and “Liabilities” (Chapter 27)
    tariffs
    taxes that governments place on imported goods (Chapter 33)
    tax incidence
    manner in which the tax burden is divided between buyers and sellers (Chapter 5)
    technological change
    a combination of invention—advances in knowledge—and innovation (Chapter 20)
    technology
    all the ways in which existing inputs produce more or higher quality, as well as different and altogether new products (Chapter 20)
    theory
    a representation of an object or situation that is simplified while including enough of the key features to help us understand the object or situation (Chapter 1)
    tight monetary policy
    see contractionary monetary policy (Chapter 28)
    time deposit
    account that the depositor has committed to leaving in the bank for a certain period of time, in exchange for a higher rate of interest; also called certificate of deposit (Chapter 27)
    Tobin taxes
    see international capital flows (Chapter 29)
    total cost
    the sum of fixed and variable costs of production (Chapter 7)
    total product
    synonym for a firm’s output (Chapter 7)
    total surplus
    see social surplus (Chapter 3)
    total utility
    satisfaction derived from consumer choices (Chapter 6)
    trade balance
    gap between exports and imports (Chapter 19)
    trade deficit
    exists when a nation's imports exceed its exports and it calculates them as imports –exports (Chapter 19)
    trade secrets
    methods of production kept secret by the producing firm (Chapter 9)
    trade surplus
    exists when a nation's exports exceed its imports and it calculates them as exports – imports (Chapter 19)
    trademark
    an identifying symbol or name for a particular good and can only be used by the firm that registered that trademark (Chapter 9)
    traditional economy
    typically an agricultural economy where things are done the same as they have always been done (Chapter 1)
    transaction costs
    the costs associated with finding a lender or a borrower for money (Chapter 27)
    Treasury bond
    a bond issued by the federal government through the U.S. Department of the Treasury (Chapter 17)
    trough
    during the business cycle, the lowest point of output in a recession, before a recovery begins (Chapter 19)
    twin deficits
    deficits that occur when a country is running both a trade and a budget deficit (Chapter 31)
    tying sales
    a situation where a customer is allowed to buy one product only if the customer also buys another product (Chapter 11)

    U¶

    underemployed
    individuals who are employed in a job that is below their skills (Chapter 21)
    underground economy
    a market where the buyers and sellers make transactions in violation of one or more government regulations (Chapter 1)
    unemployment rate
    the percentage of adults who are in the labor force and thus seeking jobs, but who do not have jobs (Chapter 21)
    unilateral transfers
    “one-way payments” that governments, private entities, or individuals make that they sent abroad with nothing received in return (Chapter 23)
    unit of account
    the common way in which we measure market values in an economy (Chapter 27)
    unitary elasticity
    when the calculated elasticity is equal to one indicating that a change in the price of the good or service results in a proportional change in the quantity demanded or supplied (Chapter 5)
    usury laws
    laws that impose an upper limit on the interest rate that lenders can charge (Chapter 4)
    utility
    satisfaction, usefulness, or value one obtains from consuming goods and services (Chapter 2)

    V¶

    value chain
    how a good is produced in stages (Chapter 33)
    variable cost
    cost of production that increases with the quantity produced; the cost of the variable inputs (Chapter 7)
    variable inputs
    factors of production that a firm can easily increase or decrease in a short period of time (Chapter 7)
    velocity
    the speed with which money circulates through the economy; calculated as the nominal GDP divided by the money supply (Chapter 28)
    venture capital
    financial investments in new companies that are still relatively small in size, but that have potential to grow substantially (Chapter 17)
    voting cycle
    the situation in which a majority prefers A over B, B over C, and C over A (Chapter 18)

    W¶

    wage elasticity of labor supply
    the percentage change in hours worked divided by the percentage change in wages (Chapter 5)
    warranty
    a promise to fix or replace the good for a certain period of time (Chapter 16)
    wealth
    the sum of the value of all assets, including money in bank accounts, financial investments, a pension fund, and the value of a home (Chapter 15)
    World Trade Organization (WTO)
    organization that seeks to negotiate reductions in barriers to trade and to adjudicate complaints about violations of international trade policy; successor to the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) (Chapter 34)

    Z¶

    zero inelasticity
    the highly inelastic case of demand or supply in which a percentage change in price, no matter how large, results in zero change in the quantity; vertical in appearance (Chapter 5)
