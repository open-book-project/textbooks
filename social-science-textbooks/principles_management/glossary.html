    Glossary¶

    The key terms of every chapter, in alphabetical order. Each entry ends with a link to the chapter where the term is defined.

    A¶

    360 assessment
    An evaluation tool that collects feedback from manager, peers, direct reports, and customers. (Chapter 11)
    ability
    The knowledge, skills, and receptiveness to learning that an individual brings to a task or job. (Chapter 14)
    abundance-based change
    Leaders assume that employees will change if they can be inspired to aim for greater degrees of excellence in their work. (Chapter 10)
    access discrimination
    A catchall term that describes when people are denied employment opportunities because of their identity group or personal characteristics such as gender, race, or age. (Chapter 12)
    access-and-legitimacy perspective
    Focuses on the benefits that a diverse workforce can bring to a business that wishes to operate within a diverse set of markets or with culturally diverse clients. (Chapter 12)
    action statements
    The means by which an organization moves forward to attain its goals. (Chapter 17)
    Adam Smith
    Adam Smith proposed the ideas of division of labor, specialization, and coordination within a corporation. (Chapter 3)
    adaptation
    Technique of working with or around differences (Chapter 15)
    Adhocracy culture
    Creates an environment of innovating, visioning the future, accepting of managing change, and risk taking, rule-breaking, experimentation, entrepreneurship, and uncertainty. (Chapter 4)
    administrative plans
    Plans that work to integrate institutional-level plans with the operating plans and tie together all of the plans created for the organization’s technical core. (Chapter 17)
    age discrimination
    Treating an employee or applicant less favorably due to their age. (Chapter 12)
    Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA)
    Forbids discrimination against individuals who are age 40 and above, including offensive or derogatory remarks that create a hostile work environment. (Chapter 12)
    Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)
    Prohibits discrimination in employment, public services, public accommodations, and telecommunications against people with disabilities. (Chapter 12)
    angel investors
    Individual investors or groups of experienced investors who provide financing for start-up businesses by investing their own funds. (Chapter 7)
    appreciative conversations
    Intense, positively framed discussions that help people to develop common ground as they work together to cocreate a positive vision of an ideal future for their organization. (Chapter 10)
    Appreciative Inquiry model
    A model specifically designed as an abundance-based, bottom-up, positive approach. (Chapter 10)
    avoidance learning
    Occurs when people learn to behave in a certain way to avoid encountering an undesired or unpleasant consequence. (Chapter 14)

    B¶

    9-box
    A matrix tool used to evaluate an organization’s talent pool based on performance and potential factors. (Chapter 11)
    barriers to entry
    Industry factors (such as high start-up costs) that can prevent new firms from successfully launching new operations in that industry. (Chapter 8)
    BCG matrix
    a tool used to evaluate the various business units in a corporation. (Chapter 9)
    benchmarking
    a performance evaluation technique where the standard for a firm’s performance is based on another firm’s superior performance. (Chapter 9)
    Born globals
    Companies that operate internationally from the day that they are created. (Chapter 6)
    boundaries
    Lines that make the limits of an area; team boundaries separate the team from its external stakeholders (Chapter 15)
    boundary conditions
    Define the degree of discretion that is available to employees for self-directed action. (Chapter 10)
    Bounded rationality
    The concept that when we make decisions, we cannot be fully rational because we don’t have all the possible information or the cognitive processing ability to make fully informed, completely rational decisions. (Chapter 2)
    Brainstorming
    A process of generating as many ideas or alternatives as possible, often in groups. (Chapter 2)
    bureaucratic model
    Max Weber’s model that states that organizations will find efficiencies when they divide the duties of labor, allow people to specialize, and create structure for coordinating their differentiated efforts within a hierarchy of responsibility. (Chapter 10)
    business ethics
    The area of applied ethics that focuses on real-world situations and the context and environment in which transactions occur. (Chapter 5)
    business plan
    A formal written statement that describes in detail the idea for a new business and how it will be carried out; includes a general description of the company, the qualifications of the owner(s), a description of the product or service, an analysis of the market, and a financial plan. (Chapter 7)
    business-level strategy
    ways that single-product firms organize their activities to succeed against rivals; at this level, include cost leadership and differentiation. (Chapter 9)
    buyer power
    In the relationship between a firm and its customers, buyers with high power can negotiate product price or features, while buyers with low power cannot. (Chapter 8)

    C¶

    capabilities
    A firm’s skill at coordinating and leveraging resources to create value. (Chapter 8)
    Carl G. Barth
    Carl G. Barth (1860–1939), mathematician, developed a slide rule for calculating how much steel to cut. (Chapter 3)
    centralization
    The concentration of control of an activity or organization under a single authority. (Chapter 10)
    change agents
    People in the organization who view themselves as agents who have discretion to act. (Chapter 10)
    change management
    The process of designing and implementing change. (Chapter 10)
    charisma
    A special personal magnetic charm or appeal that arouses loyalty and enthusiasm in a leader-follower relationship. (Chapter 13)
    charismatic leader
    A person who possesses legitimate power that arises from “exceptional sanctity, heroism, or exemplary character.” (Chapter 13)
    Chester Barnard
    Chester Barnard (1886–1961) argued that the executive purpose was to gain resources from members within the organization by ensuring that they perform their jobs and that cooperation exists between various groups within the organization. (Chapter 3)
    Christianity
    Faith based on the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of Jesus. (Chapter 6)
    Clan culture
    Focuses on relationships, team building, commitment, empowering human development, engagement, mentoring, and coaching. (Chapter 4)
    Clusters
    Representing countries that share similar cultural characteristics. (Chapter 6)
    cognitive complexity
    The ability to view situations from more than one cultural framework (Chapter 15)
    cognitive diversity
    Differences between team members regarding characteristics such as expertise, experiences, and perspectives. (Chapter 12)
    cognitive diversity hypothesis
    Multiple perspectives stemming from the cultural differences between group or organizational members result in creative problem-solving and innovation. (Chapter 12)
    collaboration
    The action of working with someone to produce or create something (Chapter 15)
    command-and-control
    The way in which people report to one another or connect to coordinate their efforts in accomplishing the work of the organization. (Chapter 10)
    communicator
    The individual, group, or organization that needs or wants to share information with another individual, group, or organization. (Chapter 16)
    Competencies
    A set of defined behaviors that an organization might utilize to define standards for success. (Chapter 11)
    Competing Values Framework
    Developed by Kim Cameron and Robert Quinn this model is used for diagnosing an organization’s cultural effectiveness and examining its fit with its environment. (Chapter 4)
    competition
    Business actions a firm undertakes to attract customers to its products and away from competitors’ products. (Chapter 8)
    competitive advantage
    When a firm successfully attracts more customers, earns more profit, or returns more value to its shareholders than rival firms do. (Chapter 8)
    competitive environment
    Factors and situations both inside the firm and outside the firm that have the potential to impact its operations and success. (Chapter 8)
    Complex Adaptive Systems (CAS)
    A model that views organizations as constantly developing and adapting to their environment, much like a living organism. (Chapter 10)
    Complex-Stable environments
    Environments that have a large number of external elements, and elements are dissimilar and where elements remain the same or change slowly. (Chapter 4)
    Complex-Unstable environments
    Environments that have a large number of external elements, and elements are dissimilar and where elements change frequently and unpredictably (Chapter 4)
    Compromise
    In a compromise, neither side gets what it wants. The best each side could get is what each can agree too. (Chapter 3)
    concurrent controls
    Controls intended to prevent deviation from a planned course of action while work is in progress. (Chapter 17)
    Confirmation bias
    The tendency to pay attention to information that confirms our existing beliefs and to ignore or discount information that conflicts with our existing beliefs. (Chapter 2)
    consideration
    A “relationship-oriented” leader behavior that is supportive, friendly, and focused on personal needs and interpersonal relationships. (Chapter 13)
    content motivation theories
    Theories that focus on what motivates people. (Chapter 14)
    contingency plans
    Plans that deal with alternative courses of action. (Chapter 17)
    Contingency school
    The contingency school explained that there were no universal laws in management, due to a wide variety of variables that influence relationships and create unique situations, and that each situation required a different response. (Chapter 3)
    contingency theory of leadership
    A theory advanced by Dr. Fred E. Fiedler that suggests that different leadership styles are effective as a function of the favorableness of the leadership situation least preferred. (Chapter 13)
    controlling
    Monitoring the behavior of organizational members and the effectiveness of the organization itself to determine whether organizational goals are being achieved and taking corrective action if necessary. (Chapter 17)
    conventional mindset
    Leaders assume that most people are inclined to resist change and therefore need to be managed in a way that encourages them to accept change. (Chapter 10)
    Corporate culture
    Defines how motivating employees’ beliefs, behaviors, relationships, and ways they work creates a culture that is based on the values the organization believes in. (Chapter 4)
    corporate culture
    The beliefs and behaviors that determine how a company’s employees and management interact inside an organization and also handle outside business transactions. Corporate culture develops organically over time from the cumulative traits of the leaders and the people that the company hires. (Chapter 5)
    corporate strategy
    the broadest level of strategy, concerned with decisions about growing, maintaining, or shrinking very large companies. (Chapter 9)
    cost-leadership strategy
    A generic business-level strategy in which a firm tightly controls costs throughout its value chain activities in order to offer customers low-priced goods and services at a profit. (Chapter 8)
    covert discrimination (interpersonal)
    An interpersonal form of discrimination that manifests in ways that are not visible or readily identifiable. (Chapter 12)
    Creativity
    The generation of new or original ideas. (Chapter 2)
    Critical thinking
    A disciplined process of evaluating the quality of information, especially by identifying logical fallacies in arguments. (Chapter 2)
    Cultural intelligence
    Refers to the individuals’ capabilities to function and manage effectively in culturally diverse settings. (Chapter 6)
    cultural intelligence
    A skill that enables individuals to function effectively in cross-cultural environments (Chapter 15)
    Cultural paradox
    Insights from an understanding of culture may not necessarily coincide with reality in that culture. (Chapter 6)
    Cultural stereotyping
    Occurs when one assumes that all people within a culture act, think, and behave the same way. (Chapter 6)
    culture change
    Involves reshaping and reimagining the core identity of the organization. (Chapter 10)
    cybernetic control
    Self-regulating control procedures. (Chapter 17)

    D¶

    debt
    A form of business financing consisting of borrowed funds that must be repaid with interest over a stated time period. (Chapter 7)
    Decision-making
    The action or process of thinking through possible options and selecting one. (Chapter 2)
    Decisional role
    One of the three major roles that a manager assumes in the organization. (Chapter 1)
    decoding
    Interpreting and understanding and making sense of a message. (Chapter 16)
    deep-level diversity
    Diversity in characteristics that are nonobservable such as attitudes, values, and beliefs, such as religion. (Chapter 12)
    defensive strategy
    a grand strategy pursued by companies facing challenges. (Chapter 9)
    deficit-based change
    Leaders assume that employees will change if they know they will otherwise face negative consequences. (Chapter 10)
    Deming cycle
    A planning model directed toward attaining continuous improvement by integrating organizational learning into the planning process (plan, do, check, act). (Chapter 17)
    demographics
    Part of PESTEL that includes facts about the income, education, age, and ethnic and racial composition of a population. (Chapter 8)
    designated leader
    The person placed in the leadership position by forces outside the group. (Chapter 13)
    Devil’s advocate
    A group member who intentionally takes on the role of being critical of the group’s ideas in order to discourage groupthink and encourage deep thought and discussion about issues prior to making decisions. (Chapter 2)
    DHL Global Connected Index
    Index tracking the flow of capital, information, trade, and human resources and representing the degree of globalization. (Chapter 6)
    differentiation
    The process of organizing employees into groups that focus on specific functions in the organization. (Chapter 10)
    differentiation strategy
    A generic business-level strategy in which firms add value to their products and services in order to attract customers who are willing to pay a higher price. (Chapter 8)
    direction
    What a person is motivated to achieve. (Chapter 14)
    disability discrimination
    Occurs when an employee or applicant is treated unfavorably due to their physical or mental disability. (Chapter 12)
    discrimination-and-fairness perspective
    A culturally diverse workforce is a moral duty that must be maintained in order to create a just and fair society. (Chapter 12)
    disturbances
    Can cause tension amongst employees, but can also be positive and a catalyst for change. (Chapter 10)
    diversified mentoring relationships
    Relationships in which the mentor and the mentee differ in terms of their status within the company and within larger society. (Chapter 12)
    diversity
    Identity-based differences among and between people that affect their lives as applicants, employees, and customers. (Chapter 12)
    Divisional structure
    An organizational structure characterized by functional departments grouped under a division head. (Chapter 4)
    Domain
    The purpose of the organization from which its strategies, organizational capabilities, resources, and management systems are mobilized to support the enterprise’s purpose. (Chapter 4)
    domain/directional planning
    The development of a course of action that moves an organization toward one domain or direction (and, therefore, away from other domains or directions). (Chapter 17)
    Dominance
    In dominance, one dictates the terms of the arrangement. (Chapter 3)

    E¶

    E-commerce
    Buying and selling of products using the Internet. (Chapter 6)
    economic factors
    PESTEL category that includes facts (such as unemployment rates, interest rates, and commodity prices) about the state of the local, national, or global economy. (Chapter 8)
    Education
    Socializing experiences that prepare individuals to act in society. (Chapter 6)
    effort-performance expectancy
    E1, the perceived probability that effort will lead to performance (or E ➨ P). (Chapter 14)
    Elton Mayo
    Elton Mayo (1880–1949) researched, theorized, and developed human relations theory based on an experiment at the Hawthorne plant on how to manage workers and to improve production. (Chapter 3)
    emergent leader
    The person who becomes a group’s leader by virtue of processes and dynamics internal to the group. (Chapter 13)
    emergent or bottom-up approach
    Organizations exist as socially constructed systems in which people are constantly making sense of and enacting an organizational reality as they interact with others in a system. (Chapter 10)
    Emerging market multinationals
    Influential companies from emerging markets that are competing head-on with established multinationals and rewriting the rules of competition by using new business models. (Chapter 6)
    Emerging markets
    Those markets in countries that present tremendous potential for multinationals. (Chapter 6)
    Emotional intelligence
    The ability to understand and manage emotions in oneself and in others. (Chapter 2)
    emotional intelligence
    The capability of individuals to recognize their own emotions and others’ emotions (Chapter 15)
    Employee life cycle
    The various stages of engagement of an employee—attraction, recruitment, onboarding, development, retention, separation. (Chapter 11)
    Employer-employee relationship
    The employment relationship; the legal link between employers and employees that exists when a person performs work or services under specific conditions in return for payment. (Chapter 11)
    encoding
    Translating a message into symbols or language that a receiver can understand. (Chapter 16)
    entrepreneurial activities
    The implementation of new ventures and idea generation in organizations. (Chapter 18)
    entrepreneurs
    People with vision, drive, and creativity who are willing to take the risk of starting and managing a business to make a profit, or greatly changing the scope and direction of an existing firm. (Chapter 7)
    entrepreneurship
    The process of designing, launching, and running a new business. (Chapter 10)
    environmental factors
    PESTEL category that examines a firm’s external situation with respect to the natural environment, including pollution, natural resource availability and preservation, and alternative energy. (Chapter 8)
    environmental scanning
    The systematic and intentional analysis of a firm’s internal state and its external environment. (Chapter 8)
    Equal Employment Opportunity Commission
    An organization that enforces laws and issues guidelines for employment-related treatment according to Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. (Chapter 12)
    Equal Pay Act of 1963
    An amendment to the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938. (Chapter 12)
    equity
    A form of business financing consisting of funds raised through the sale of stock (i.e., ownership) in a business. (Chapter 7)
    equity theory
    States that human motivation is affected by the outcomes people receive for their inputs, compared to the outcomes and inputs of other people. (Chapter 14)
    ERG theory
    Compresses Maslow’s five need categories into three: existence, relatedness, and growth. (Chapter 14)
    Escalation of commitment
    The tendency of decision makers to remain committed to poor decision, even when doing so leads to increasingly negative outcomes. (Chapter 2)
    ethical dilemma
    A situation in which a difficult choice has to be made between two courses of action with ethical consequences. (Chapter 5)
    ethical relativism
    Holds that people set their own moral standards for judging their actions, based on self-interest. (Chapter 5)
    ethics
    The code of moral principles and values that governs the behaviors of a person or group with respect to what is right or wrong. (Chapter 5)
    Evidence-based decision-making
    A process of collecting the best available evidence prior to making a decision. (Chapter 2)
    executive managers
    Generally, a team of individuals at the highest level of management of an organization. (Chapter 1)
    exit
    Technique of last resort—removal of a team member (Chapter 15)
    Expatriate
    Foreign employee who moves and works in another country for an extended period of time. (Chapter 6)
    expectancy theory
    Posits that people will exert high effort levels to perform at high levels so that they can obtain valued outcomes. (Chapter 14)
    explicit knowledge
    Information codified or written down as rules or guidelines. (Chapter 18)
    Exporting
    International entry mode where a company sends a product to an international market and fills the order like a domestic order. (Chapter 6)
    external environment
    The aspects of the world at large and of a firm’s industry that can impact its operations. (Chapter 8)
    external factors
    Things in the world or industry environments that may impact a firm’s operations or success, such as the economy, government actions, or supplier power. Strategic decisions can be made in response to these things but normally cannot directly influence or change them. (Chapter 8)
    extinction
    Occurs when a consequence or lack of a consequence makes it less likely that a behavior will be repeated in the future. (Chapter 14)
    extrinsic motivation
    Occurs when a person performs a given behavior to acquire something that will satisfy a lower-order need. (Chapter 14)
    extrinsic outcomes
    Are awarded or given by other people (like a supervisor). (Chapter 14)

    F¶

    Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA)
    Provides new parents, including adoptive and foster parents, with 12 weeks of unpaid leave (or paid leave only if earned by the employee) to care for the new child and requires that nursing mothers have the right to express milk on workplace premises. (Chapter 12)
    figurehead role
    A necessary role for a manager who wants to inspire people within the organization to feel connected to each other and to the institution, to support the policies and decisions made on behalf of the organization, and to work harder for the good of the institution. (Chapter 16)
    first-line management
    The level of management directly managing nonmanagerial employees. (Chapter 1)
    Five functions of management
    Created by Henri Fayol: planning, organizing, staffing, controlling, and directing. (Chapter 3)
    flat organization
    A horizontal organizational structure in which many individuals across the whole system are empowered to make organizational decisions. (Chapter 10)
    focus strategy
    A generic business-level competitive strategy that firms use in combination with either a cost-leadership or differentiation strategy in order to target a smaller demographic or geographic market with specialized products or services. (Chapter 8)
    followship
    The process of seeking or accepting influence. (Chapter 18)
    Foreign direct investment (FDI)
    Refers to deliberate efforts of a country or company to invest in another country through the form of ownership positions in companies in another country. (Chapter 6)
    Foreign direct investment (FDI)
    Involves a company investing in another country through the construction of facilities and buildings in another country. (Chapter 6)
    formal and informal contracts
    Used to allow firms to share technology between each other. (Chapter 18)
    formal leader
    That individual who is recognized by those outside the group as the official leader of the group. (Chapter 13)
    formal organization
    A fixed set of rules of organizational procedures and structures. (Chapter 10)
    formalization
    The process of making a status formal for the practice of formal acceptance. (Chapter 10)
    Forming
    The first stage of team development—the positive and polite stage (Chapter 15)
    franchise agreements
    Long-term agreements that involve long payoffs for the sharing of known technology. (Chapter 18)
    Frank and Lillian Gilbreth
    Their major contribution was motion studies. (Chapter 3)
    Frederick Winslow Taylor
    Taylor is known as the father of scientific management. (Chapter 3)
    Functional structure
    The earliest and most used organizational designs. (Chapter 4)

    G¶

    generic business-level strategies
    Basic methods of organizing firm value chain activities to compete in a product market that can be used by any sized firm in any industry. (Chapter 8)
    Geographic structure
    An Organizational option aimed at moving from a mechanistic to more organic design to serve customers faster and with relevant products and services; as such, this structure is organized by locations of customers that a company serves. (Chapter 4)
    geographic structures
    Occur when organizations are set up to deliver a range of products within a geographic area or region. (Chapter 10)
    glass ceiling
    An invisible barrier based on the prejudicial beliefs of organizational decision makers that prevents women from moving beyond certain levels within a company. (Chapter 12)
    Global strategy
    Where all operations and activities are managed fairly similarly worldwide. (Chapter 6)
    Globalization
    Worldwide phenomenon whereby the countries of the world are becoming more interconnected and where trade barriers are disappearing. (Chapter 6)
    GLOBE project
    More recent cultural project involving 170 researchers who collected data on 17,000 managers from 62 countries around the world. (Chapter 6)
    goal
    something that a firm is trying to accomplish; can also be called an objective. (Chapter 9)
    goal commitment
    The degree to which people dedicate themselves to achieving a goal. (Chapter 14)
    goal hierarchy
    The interrelationship among an organization’s job-, department-, divisional-, and organizational-level goals. (Chapter 17)
    goal planning
    Development of action statements to move toward the attainment of a specific goal. (Chapter 17)
    goal theory
    States that people will perform better if they have difficult, specific, accepted performance goals or objectives. (Chapter 14)
    Government and political environment forces
    The global economy and changing political actions increase uncertainty for businesses, while creating opportunities for some industries and instability in others. (Chapter 4)
    great man theory of leadership
    The belief that some people are born to be leaders and others are not. (Chapter 13)
    ground rules
    Basic rules or principles of conduct that govern a situation or endeavor (Chapter 15)
    group-level change
    Centers on the relationships between people and focuses on helping people to work more effectively together. (Chapter 10)
    Groupthink
    The tendency of a group to reach agreement very quickly and without substantive discussion. (Chapter 2)
    groupthink
    A dysfunction in decision-making that is common in homogeneous groups due to group pressures and group members’ desire for conformity and consensus. (Chapter 12)
    growth strategy
    a grand strategy to increase the size of the firm in terms of revenue, market share, geographic reach, or a combination of these elements. (Chapter 9)

    H¶

    Hammurabi
    The Code of Hammurabi was a listing of 282 laws that regulated conduct on a wide variety of behaviors, including business dealings, personnel behavior, interpersonal relations, punishments and a wide variety of other outcomes. (Chapter 3)
    harassment
    Any unwelcome conduct that is based on characteristics such as age, race, national origin, disability, gender, or pregnancy status. (Chapter 12)
    head, body, and heart
    Techniques for becoming more adept in cross-cultural skills—learning about cultures (head), physical manifestations of culture (body), and emotional commitment to new culture (heart) (Chapter 15)
    hedonism
    Assumes that people are motivated to satisfy mainly their own needs (seek pleasure, avoid pain). (Chapter 14)
    Henri Fayol
    Fayol’s administrative theory was the first general statement on management theory. (Chapter 3)
    Henry Gantt
    Henry Gantt (1861–1919) developed the Gantt chart, which allowed for the process of control to occur. (Chapter 3)
    Heuristics
    Mental shortcuts that allow a decision maker to reach a good decision quickly. They are strategies that develop based on prior experience. (Chapter 2)
    hidden diversity
    Differences in traits that are deep-level and may be concealed or revealed at discretion by individuals who possess them. (Chapter 12)
    Hierarchy culture
    Emphasizes efficiency, process and cost control, organizational improvement, technical expertise, precision, problem solving, elimination of errors, logical, cautious and conservative, management and operational analysis, careful decision making. (Chapter 4)
    High-rigor cross-cultural training
    Methods of training where participants are much more actively engaged in the training process and can learn some tacit aspects of cross-cultural differences. (Chapter 6)
    highly structured interviews
    Interviews that are be structured objectively to remove bias from the selection process. (Chapter 12)
    Hinduism
    Represented by all those who honor the ancient scriptures called the Vedas. (Chapter 6)
    Hofstede model of national culture
    Project involving survey of over 88,000 employees in IBM subsidiaries from 72 countries. (Chapter 6)
    horizontal organizational structure
    Flat organizational structure in which many individuals across the whole system are empowered to make organizational decisions. (Chapter 10)
    Horizontal organizational structures
    A “flatter” organizational structure often found in matrix organizations where individuals relish the breath and development that their team offers. (Chapter 4)
    Human capital
    The skills, knowledge, and experience of an individual or group, and that value to an organization. (Chapter 11)
    Human resource management
    The management of people within organizations, focusing on the touchpoints of the employee life cycle. (Chapter 11)
    Human resources compliance
    The HR role to ensure adherence to laws and regulations that govern the employment relationship. (Chapter 11)
    hybrid control system
    Control system that exercises control prior to, during, and after the performance of a work activity. (Chapter 17)
    hybrid planning
    The coupling of domain and goal planning. (Chapter 17)
    hygienes
    Factors in the work environment that are based on the basic human need to “avoid pain.” (Chapter 14)

    I¶

    identity group
    A collective of individuals who share the same demographic characteristics such as race, sex, or age. (Chapter 12)
    Immigration
    Movement of people from their home country to other countries; will continue to grow worldwide. (Chapter 6)
    implementation
    the execution of a strategy by planning and assigning actions to employees to carry out in order to accomplish the company’s strategic objectives. (Chapter 9)
    inclusion
    The degree to which employees are accepted and treated fairly by their organization. (Chapter 12)
    incremental change
    Small refinements in current organizational practices or routines that do not challenge, but rather build on or improve, existing aspects and practices within the organization. (Chapter 10)
    individual-level change
    Focuses on how to help employees to improve some active aspect of their performance or the knowledge they need to continue to contribute to the organization in an effective manner. (Chapter 10)
    Individualism
    Degree to which a society focuses on the relationship of the individual to the group. (Chapter 6)
    Industrial Revolution
    The Industrial Revolution occurred between roughly 1760 and 1900 and saw the emergence of the modern factory. (Chapter 3)
    industry
    A group of firms all offering products or services in a single category, for example restaurants or athletic equipment. (Chapter 8)
    industry rivalry
    One of Porter’s Five Forces; refers to the intensity of competition between firms in an industry. (Chapter 8)
    informal leader
    That individual whom members of the group acknowledge as their leader. (Chapter 13)
    informal organization
    The connecting social structure in organizations that denotes the evolving network of interactions among its employees, unrelated to the firm's formal authority structure. (Chapter 10)
    Informational role
    One of the three major roles that a manager assumes in the organization. (Chapter 1)
    initiating structure
    A “task-oriented” leader behavior that is focused on goal attainment, organizing and scheduling work, solving problems, and maintaining work processes. (Chapter 13)
    innovation
    Invention, new product development, and process-improvement methods are all examples of innovation. (Chapter 18)
    input
    Any personal qualities that a person views as having value and that are relevant to the organization. (Chapter 14)
    instincts
    Our natural, fundamental needs, basic to our survival. (Chapter 14)
    instrumental values
    The preferred means of behavior used to obtain desired goals. (Chapter 5)
    Integration

    Popular policy instruments that countries agree on to eliminate cross-border barriers to trade and to promote global integration. (Chapter 6)
    Training, stretch assignments, individual assessments, individual development plans
    These are tools that may be used in talent development: Training—a forum for learning in person or online Stretch assignments—challenge roles for high-potential employees Individual assessments—personality and work style inventories of employees Individual development plans—documents that highlight an individual employee’s opportunities for growth and path of action (Chapter 11)
    transformational change
    Significant shifts in an organizational system that may cause significant disruption to some underlying aspect of the organization, its processes, or its structures. (Chapter 10)
    transformational leader
    A leader who moves and changes things “in a big way” by inspiring others to perform the extraordinary. (Chapter 13)
    treatment discrimination
    A situation in which people are employed but are treated differently while employed, mainly by receiving different and unequal job-related opportunities or rewards. (Chapter 12)

    U¶

    Uncertainty avoidance
    Refers to the degree to which people in a society are comfortable with uncertainty and unpredictable situations. (Chapter 6)
    underreward inequity
    Occurs when people perceive their outcome/input ratio to be less than that of their referent other. (Chapter 14)
    Unity of command
    Unity of command stresses that each worker should have only one supervisor. (Chapter 3)
    unplanned change
    An unintentional activity that is usually the result of informal organizing. (Chapter 10)
    Uppsala model of internationalization
    Model that argues that as firms learn more about a specific market, they become more committed by investing more resources into that market. (Chapter 6)

    V¶

    valences
    The degree to which a person perceives an outcome as being desirable, neutral, or undesirable. (Chapter 14)
    value chain
    Sequence of activities that firms perform to turn inputs (parts or supplies) into outputs (goods or services). (Chapter 8)
    value proposition
    A promise by a company to a customer or market segment. (Chapter 18)
    venture capital
    Financing obtained from venture capitalists, investment firms that specialize in financing small, high-growth companies and receive an ownership interest and a voice in management in return for their money. (Chapter 7)
    vertical organizational structure
    Organizational structures found in large mechanistic organizations; also called “tall” structures due to the presence of many levels of management. (Chapter 10)
    Virtual structure
    A recent organizational structure that has emerged in the 1990’s and early 2000’s as a response to requiring more flexibility, solution based tasks on demand, less geographical constraints, and accessibility to dispersed expertise. (Chapter 4)
    virtue ethics
    Grounded in one’s character, focusing on what type of person one ought to be. (Chapter 5)
    vision statement
    a broad expression of what a business’s founders want that business to accomplish. (Chapter 9)
    visionary leader
    A leader who influences others through an emotional and/or intellectual attraction to the leader’s dreams of what “can be.” (Chapter 13)
    VRIO
    analytical tool that evaluates a firm’s resources and capabilities to determine whether or not it can support an advantage for the firm in the competitive environment: value, rarity, imitation, and organization. (Chapter 8)

    W¶

    War for talent
    Coined by McKinsey & Company in 1997, it refers to the increasing competition for recruiting and retaining talented employees. (Chapter 11)
    weaknesses
    Things that a firm does not have good capabilities to perform or gaps in firm resources. (Chapter 8)
    work motivation
    The amount of effort a person exerts to achieve a level of job performance (Chapter 14)
    work visa
    A temporary documented status that authorizes individuals from other countries to permanently or temporarily live and work in the United States. (Chapter 12)
    working group
    Group of experts working together to achieve specific goals; performance is made up of the individual results of all members (Chapter 15)
    workplace discrimination
    Unfair treatment in the job hiring process or at work that is based on the identity group, physical or mental condition, or personal characteristic of an applicant or employee. (Chapter 12)

    Z¶

    Zone of indifference
    Workers would comply with orders if they were indifferent to them. This does not mean they have to agree with the orders. Rather the zone of indifference suggests that workers need merely to be indifferent to an order to follow it. (Chapter 3)
