    Glossary¶

    The key terms of every chapter, in alphabetical order. Each entry ends with a link to the chapter where the term is defined.

    A¶

    A posteriori knowledge
    knowledge gained through experience. (Chapter 7)
    A priori knowledge
    knowledge that can be gained prior to or independent of experience. (Chapter 7)
    Abductive
    having to do with abduction/abductive reasoning. Abduction is probabilistic form of inference in which an explanation is offered to justify and explain evidence. (Chapter 5)
    Abortion
    the intentional ending of a pregnancy. (Chapter 10)
    act utilitarianism
    a utilitarian approach that proposes that people should apply the greatest happiness principle on a case-by-case basis (Chapter 9)
    Active euthanasia
    a form of euthanasia in which a patient’s life is terminated using medical interventions (e.g., administering a lethal dose of medication). (Chapter 10)
    Actuality
    in Aristotelian thought, the level to which a being has realized its purpose. (Chapter 6)
    Ad hominin attack
    fallacy of relevance that argues against someone’s idea or suggestion by attacking the individual personally, rather than pointing out problems with the idea or suggestion. (Chapter 5)
    Ahimsa
    one of the highest virtues of classical Indian religions. It is the practice of refraining from harming other living things. (Chapter 1)
    Ahimsa
    a foundational principle in Indian philosophy to refrain from harming oneself or others. (Chapter 11)
    Alienation
    in Marxism, the estrangement of workers from their work and from themselves due to capitalist exploitation. (Chapter 11)
    Allostasis
    the biological process whereby the body prepares itself for anticipated needs. (Chapter 2)
    Altruism
    the selfless care for others’ well-being. (Chapter 8)
    Anarchism
    a state of no governance or political oversight. (Chapter 11)
    Anatman
    a Buddhist concept of the self as no-self (as not retaining identity through time). (Chapter 6)
    Anchoring bias
    the tendency to make estimates based on an earlier initial value. (Chapter 2)
    Anthropocentric
    human-centered. (Chapter 10)
    Anthropogenic climate change
    changes in Earth’s climate caused or influenced by human activity. (Chapter 10)
    Anti-realism
    the philosophical position that argues that morality is subjective, not objective. (Chapter 8)
    Appeal to ignorance
    a fallacy of weak induction that relies on the lack of knowledge or evidence for a thing (our ignorance of it) to draw a definite conclusion about that thing. (Chapter 5)
    applied ethics
    a branch of ethics that focuses on the application of moral norms to determine the permissibility of specific actions (Chapter 9)
    Applied ethics
    an area of ethics that focuses on the application of moral norms and principles to controversial issues to determine the rightness of specific actions. (Chapter 10)
    Argument
    a set of sentences, where some of those sentences (called premises) provide support for another sentence, called the conclusion. (Chapter 1)
    Argument
    a set of reasons offered in support of a conclusion. (Chapter 5)
    Aristocracy
    a class of people considered to be elite members of society. (Chapter 11)
    Arête
    the ancient Greek word for virtue. It can also be translated as “excellence.” (Chapter 8)
    Ataraxia
    the goal of Epicurus’s hedonism: tranquility, or freedom from mental, emotional, and physical pain. (Chapter 8)
    Availability heuristic
    the tendency to evaluate new information based on the most recent or most easily recalled examples. (Chapter 2)

    B¶

    Bandwagon fallacy
    the fallacy that we ought to do something or believe something because many other people do or believe the same thing. (Chapter 2)
    Begging the question
    a fallacy of unwarranted assumption that either assumes the truth of a conclusion in the course of trying to prove it or assumes the truth of a contentious claim. (Chapter 5)
    Biased sample
    a fallacy of weak induction that draws a conclusion using evidence that is biased in some way. (Chapter 5)
    Bioethics
    a field that studies ethical issues that emerge with advances in biology, technology, and medicine. (Chapter 10)

    C¶

    Capital
    money and commodities. (Chapter 11)
    care ethics
    an approach to ethics that emphasizes the importance of subjective factors, specifics of concrete situations, and the relationships of individuals (Chapter 9)
    Categorical imperative
    Kant’s concept of moral reasoning and action. “Act only according to that maxim whereby you can, at the same time, will that it should become a universal law” (Kant [1785] 1998, 31). This means you know an action is moral if can be universal for everyone. (Chapter 8)
    categorical imperative
    a moral law that individuals have a duty to follow and that is rationally devised through Kant’s four formulations (Chapter 9)
    Clinical trials
    trials designed to test new medical interventions and establish a drug’s dosage, determine possible side effects, and demonstrate efficacy. (Chapter 10)
    Codices
    singular codex; Maya books that transmitted the collective mathematical, scientific, historical, religious, and metaphysical knowledge of the Maya. (Chapter 3)
    Cognitive bias
    a systematic pattern of reasoning that deviates from a rationally optimal or logical judgment based on available facts and probabilities. (Chapter 2)
    Cognitive science
    the study of the brain and the mechanisms underlying thought, perception, memory, emotion, and other functions of the brain. (Chapter 2)
    Cognitivism
    the philosophical position that values are cognitive and express statements about properties of things or states of events. (Chapter 8)
    Coherence
    a situation in which it is possible for a set of beliefs or statements to be true at the same time. (Chapter 1)
    Coherentism
    the theory that a belief is justified if it is part of a coherent system of interconnected beliefs. (Chapter 7)
    Common sense
    knowledge primarily derived from perception that seems clearly or obviously true. (Chapter 1)
    Communicative action
    a term coined by Jürgen Habermas to refer to open discussion within a public forum, with the potential to change political systems and societies. (Chapter 12)
    Compassion
    the ability to care or share in others’ suffering. (Chapter 8)
    Compatibilism
    the view that a lack of freedom for the human moral agent is compatible with moral culpability for that same agent. (Chapter 6)
    Conceptual analysis
    the process of taking apart and making sense of sentences or claims by examining their component parts. (Chapter 1)
    Conclusion
    the result of an argument. A conclusion is that which is meant to be proved by the reasoning and premises used in an argument. (Chapter 5)
    Conditional
    a logical statement that expresses a necessary and a sufficient condition. Conditionals are usually formulated as if–then statements. (Chapter 5)
    Confirmation bias
    the tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and recall information that confirms or supports established beliefs. (Chapter 2)
    Confucianism
    a normative moral theory developed in ancient China during the Warring States period that proposes that the development of individual character is key to the achievement of an ethical and harmonious society. (Chapter 3)
    Confucianism
    a normative moral theory that arose in ancient China during the Warring States period that proposes the development of individual character is key to the achievement of an ethical and harmonious society (Chapter 9)
    Conscience
    an individual’s inner sense of right and wrong. (Chapter 8)
    consequentialism
    a moral theory that looks at an action’s outcome or consequences to determine whether it is morally right (Chapter 9)
    Contextualism
    the view that the truth of knowledge attributions depends on the context. (Chapter 7)
    Contextualist approach
    an approach to the philosophy that interprets the ideas of philosophers in terms of the historical and cultural contexts in which they wrote. (Chapter 4)
    Contradiction
    a statement that is always false. A contradiction is the conjunction of any statement and its negation. (Chapter 5)
    Cosmological argument
    a type of argument for the existence of God based upon consideration of cosmic causality. (Chapter 6)
    Counterexample
    an example that proves that either a statement is false or an argument is invalid. (Chapter 5)
    Critical pedagogy
    the application of the insights of critical theory to pedagogy; the belief that all education should be in service of disrupting oppressive systems of power in all their forms. (Chapter 12)
    Critical race theory
    approaches the concept of race as a social construct and examines how race has been defined by the power structure. (Chapter 12)
    Critical theory
    any method of assessing and challenging the power structures of societies; also refers to the various theoretical approaches to assessing and challenging power structures associated with the Institute for Social Research (Frankfurt School). (Chapter 12)

    D¶

    Dao
    in Confucianism, ethical principles or a path by which to live; in Daoism, the natural way of the universe and all things. (Chapter 3)
    dao
    in Confucianism, ethical principles or path by which to live life; in Daoism, the natural way of the universe and all things (Chapter 9)
    Daoism
    a belief system developed in ancient China that encourages the practice of living in accordance with the dao, the natural way of the universe and all things. (Chapter 3)
    Daoism
    a belief system developed in ancient China that encourages the practice of living in accordance with the dao, the natural way of the universe and all things (Chapter 9)
    Darshana
    a way of beholding the sacred or manifestations of the divine in Hindu thought. (Chapter 3)
    Deconstruction
    a method of connecting the meaning of a text to the social forces at play in its creation; a strategy for analyzing the ways in which humans create objects and essential ideas where they don’t naturally exist. (Chapter 12)
    Deductive
    having to do with deduction/deductive reasoning. Deduction is a form of inference that can guarantee the truth of its conclusions, given the truth of the premises. (Chapter 5)
    Deep ecology
    an approach to environmental ethics that assumes all living things are valuable in their own right and not only because of their usefulness. (Chapter 10)
    Definite description
    a method of conceptual analysis that substitutes a descriptive phrase that uniquely identifies the object or thing named for an object term or proper name. (Chapter 1)
    Democracy
    government either by elected representatives of the people or directly by the people themselves. (Chapter 11)
    Deontologist
    someone who believes that ethical actions follow universal moral laws. (Chapter 10)
    deontology
    a moral theory that focuses on duties or rules to determine the rightness of an action (Chapter 9)
    Descriptive claims
    statements that describe matters of fact or how the world is. (Chapter 8)
    Determinism
    the belief that human actions are governed by the laws of nature. (Chapter 6)
    Dialectic
    a method of discovering truth that comes from dialogue and uses the exchange of different points of view to arrive at a position that is more likely to be true. (Chapter 2)
    Dialectic method
    Hegel’s understanding of history as a movement created by the interaction between a thesis (an original state) and a force countering that original state (antithesis), resulting in a new and higher state (synthesis). (Chapter 12)
    Dialectical materialism
    a revision of Hegel’s dialectic method proposed by Karl Marx, which identities the contradictions within material, real-world phenomena as the driving force of historical change. (Chapter 12)
    Discourse
    the process of making meaning out of texts and dialogues. (Chapter 12)
    Divine command theory
    the philosophical position that uses God as the principle for morality. What is good is determined by God’s commands. (Chapter 8)
    Divine rule
    a doctrine of political authority in which the legitimacy of the monarch or ruler is derived from the will of the divine. (Chapter 11)
    Dualism
    a view that posits two types of being in order to account fully for the nature of the thing under scrutiny. (Chapter 6)
    Dunning-Kruger effect
    the cognitive bias in which people with little expertise in a specific task rate their knowledge too highly relative to others with more knowledge. (Chapter 2)

    E¶

    Egalitarianism
    the notion that all individuals enjoy equal status and moral worth and that any legitimate system of government should reflect this in its policies and procedures. (Chapter 11)
    Emotional appeal
    fallacy of relevance that appeals to feelings (whether positive or negative) rather than discussing the merits of an idea or proposal. (Chapter 5)
    Emotivism
    a branch of non-cognitivism that argues that value judgments only express emotion. (Chapter 8)
    Empathy
    the ability to share others’ feelings. (Chapter 8)
    Empiricism
    a belief that all knowledge is derived inductively from sense experience. (Chapter 4)
    Ensoulment
    the point in time when a developing life is believed to possess a soul. (Chapter 10)
    Enumeration
    the listing of the component parts of a concept, notion, or thing. (Chapter 1)
    Environmental ethics
    an area of applied ethics that attempts to rethink our relationship to the natural world and identify right conduct in our dealings with the nonhuman world. (Chapter 10)
    Epistemic humility
    a stance in philosophical and scientific investigation that recognizes the limits of one’s own ability to know truth and reality in a direct or complete way. (Chapter 2)
    Epistemic injustice
    injustice that arises from or is related to epistemological issues. (Chapter 7)
    Epistemic peer
    a person who is in an equal epistemic position as you relative to some domain. (Chapter 7)
    Epistemology
    the study of knowledge, involving questions such as how humans know what they know, what is the nature of true knowledge, and what are the limits to what humans can know. (Chapter 3)
    Epistemology
    the field within philosophy that focuses on questions pertaining to the nature and extent of human knowledge. (Chapter 7)
    essentialism
    a view that a set of characteristics makes something what it is (Chapter 9)
    Ethical naturalism
    the philosophical position that argues that moral values are based on natural facts about the world, not individuals’ subjective feelings or beliefs. (Chapter 8)
    ethics
    the field of philosophy that investigates morality (Chapter 9)
    Ethnophilosophy
    the study of the philosophies of Indigenous peoples. (Chapter 3)
    Eudaimonia
    the ancient Greek term for “happiness” or “human flourishing.” It literally means “good” (eu) “spirit” (daimon). (Chapter 8)
    eudaimonia
    the flourishing life, which ancient Greek philosophers (e.g., Aristotle, the Stoics, and Epicurus) set as the aim of life (Chapter 9)
    Euthanasia
    means “good death” and refers to the ending of a human life to avoid suffering. (Chapter 10)
    Euthyphro problem
    a challenge to theistic ethical systems. It asks whether something is good because God commands it or if God commands it because it is good. (Chapter 8)
    Evaluative claims
    statements that express a judgment about something’s value or how the world ought to be. (Chapter 8)
    Everyday aesthetics
    an approach to aesthetic theory that focuses on aesthetically meaningful experiences in people’s ordinary day-to-day lives. (Chapter 8)
    Experience machine
    a thought experiment in which the possibility is raised that a person might lead a pleasurable life by being plugged into a machine stimulating pleasurable experiences in their brain. (Chapter 8)
    Experimental philosophy
    philosophy that uses methods from experimental science to test claims made in philosophy. (Chapter 1)
    Explanatory virtues
    aspects of an explanation that generally make it strong; four such virtues are that a good hypothesis should be explanatory, simple, and conservative, and have depth. (Chapter 5)
    Externalism
    any epistemological theory that does not solely use a subjects’ mental states to determine justification. (Chapter 7)
    Extrinsic value
    the quality of being valued for the sake of something else. (Chapter 8)

    F¶

    Fact-value distinction
    the distinction between what is the case (facts) and what people think ought to be the case (values) based on beliefs about what is good, beautiful, important, etc. (Chapter 8)
    Faith
    beliefs that are not or cannot be proven. (Chapter 8)
    Fallacy
    a poor form of reasoning. (Chapter 5)
    Fallacy
    an error in logical reasoning—for example, jumping to a conclusion without proper evidence. (Chapter 8)
    Fallacy of diversion
    a general category of informal fallacies in which an arguer presents evidence that functions to divert the attention of the audience from the current subject of argument. (Chapter 5)
    Fallacy of relevance
    a general category of informal fallacies in which an arguer relies on reasons that are not relevant for establishing a conclusion. (Chapter 5)
    Fallacy of unwarranted assumption
    a general category of informal fallacies in which an arguer implicitly or explicitly relies on reasons that require further justification. (Chapter 5)
    Fallacy of weak induction
    a general category of informal fallacies in which an arguer’s evidence or reasons are too weak to firmly establish their conclusion. (Chapter 5)
    False cause
    fallacy of weak induction in which a causal relation is assumed to exist between two events or things that are not causally connected; “correlation does not equal causation”. (Chapter 5)
    False dichotomy
    a fallacy of unwarranted assumption in which a limited number of possibilities are assumed to be the only available options. (Chapter 5)
    femininity
    a social construct that categorizes specific traits as female and establishes society’s expectation of women (Chapter 9)
    feminism
    a political and philosophical movement that aims to end sexism and promote social justice for women (Chapter 9)
    Feminist care ethics
    an ethical theory that proposes that morality is based on caring for others and that caring for others arises out of women’s experiences as caregivers. (Chapter 8)
    Filial piety
    the ethical obligation of children to their parents. (Chapter 3)
    Forms
    the means by which an invisible, unchanging creator gives rise to the material world that we live in. (Chapter 10)
    Foundation
    a principle, concept, or assumption on which a philosophical position is founded. (Chapter 8)
    Foundationalism
    the belief that all truth is either self-evident or derivable from some truth that is self-evident. (Chapter 7)
    Frankfurt School
    another name for the Institute for Social Research at the University of Frankfurt; also refers to an amalgam of thinkers affiliated with the Institute for Social Research. (Chapter 12)
    Fundamentality
    the issue of foundations, the philosophical inquiry into the basis for an idea or system of ideas. (Chapter 8)

    G¶

    Gambler’s fallacy
    the reasoning that holds that if a chance event has happened less frequently in the recent past, it is more likely to happen in the near future (or vice versa). (Chapter 2)
    gender binarism
    the view that each person can be categorized by one of two genders (male or female) (Chapter 9)
    Germ-line interventions
    inheritable genetic modification. (Chapter 10)
    Gettier case
    a case, usually presented as a hypothetical scenario, that acts as a counterexample to the traditional account of knowledge as justified true belief. (Chapter 7)
    Global skeptic
    someone who rejects the possibility of knowledge in general. (Chapter 7)
    good will
    the capacity to be a good person (Chapter 9)
    greatest happiness principle
    a principle that holds that actions are right when they produce the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people (Chapter 9)

    H¶

    Hasty generalization
    fallacy of weak induction that draws a conclusion using too little evidence to support the conclusion. (Chapter 5)
    Hedonism
    a philosophical approach to moral theory based on the idea that pleasure dictates what is good and pain dictates what is bad. (Chapter 8)
    Hermeneutic approach
    an approach to philosophy that takes the historical context of the original text seriously but also recognizes the influence of contemporary issues and perspectives. (Chapter 4)
    Hermeneutical injustice
    a type of epistemic injustice that occurs when a society’s language and concepts cannot adequately capture the experience of people, thereby limiting understanding of their experiences. (Chapter 7)
    Hermeneutics
    the study and theory of interpretation of texts, including not only a linguistic analysis but also a background investigation into how the context that gives birth to a text affects how it can and should be interpreted. (Chapter 12)
    Heuristics
    mental shortcuts or rules of thumb that provide a method of problem-solving that is not necessarily optimal but is efficient. (Chapter 2)
    higher pleasures
    pleasures associated with the exercise of a person’s higher faculties (e.g., the use of higher cognitive faculties and/or participation in social/cultural life) (Chapter 9)
    Historical reliabilism
    an epistemological theory that proposes that processes that reliably produce true beliefs confer justification on those beliefs. (Chapter 7)
    Historicity
    the process of verification of the events said to be historical. (Chapter 12)
    Homeostasis
    the biological process whereby the body regulates itself to maintain a state of equilibrium. (Chapter 2)
    Human augmentation
    refers to attempts to enhance or increase human capabilities through technological, biomedical, or other interventions. (Chapter 10)
    humanity formulation
    a rational method of devising moral laws that specifies that each person be treated as an end, never merely as a means (Chapter 9)
    Hylomorphism
    the idea that being is composed of matter and form that causes the being to actualize its potential. (Chapter 10)
    Hypothesis
    a proposed explanation for an observed process or phenomenon. (Chapter 5)
    hypothetical imperative
    a rule that needs to be followed in order to achieve some (proposed) end (Chapter 9)

    I¶

    Ikigai
    reason for being; what makes life meaningful in an intuitive way. (Chapter 8)
    incidental friendship
    casual relationships that are based on utility or pleasure (Chapter 9)
    Incommensurability
    when there is no standard of evaluation between two or more goods or values. (Chapter 8)
    Indigenous philosophy
    the ideas of Indigenous peoples pertaining to the nature of the world, human existence, ethics, ideal social and political structures, and other topics also considered by traditional academic philosophy. (Chapter 3)
    Inductive
    having to do with induction/inductive reasoning. Induction is a probabilistic form of inference in which observation or experience is used to draw conclusions about the world. (Chapter 5)
    Inference
    the mental process that leads from one set of information (premises, data, or information) to another (a conclusion, construction, or projection). (Chapter 2)
    Inference
    a reasoning process that moves from one idea to another, resulting in conclusions. (Chapter 5)
    Institutional review boards (IRBs)
    committees tasked with reviewing and vetting parameters of trials to protect participants and identify potential issues. (Chapter 10)
    Instrumental value
    possessing value as a means to something else or for the sake of something else. (Chapter 10)
    Intentional fallacy
    the faulty argument that the intention of the artist determines the meaning of the work of art. (Chapter 8)
    Internalism
    any epistemological theory that focuses solely on subjects’ mental states to determine justification. (Chapter 7)
    intersectionality
    different aspects of identity (e.g., gender, race, sexuality, and class) that intersect in a person’s identity and define or influence their lived experience (Chapter 9)
    Intrinsic value
    the quality of being valued for its own sake. (Chapter 8)
    Intrinsic value
    possessing value in itself or for its own sake. (Chapter 10)
    Intuition
    certain and evident cognition; the kind of knowledge that is so clear that it seems impossible for it to be false. (Chapter 1)
    Intuition
    cognition that seems completely self-evident and impossible to deny. (Chapter 8)
    Invalidity
    a property of bad deductive inferences. An invalid inference/argument is one in which the truth of the premises does not guarantee the truth of the conclusion. (Chapter 5)
    Is-ought problem
    problem that asserts the challenge of moving from statements of fact (something is) to statements of value (something ought to be). (Chapter 8)

    J¶

    Junzi
    in Confucianism, a person who is an exemplary ethical figure and lives according to the dao. (Chapter 3)
    junzi
    in Confucianism, a person who is an exemplary ethical figure and lives according to the dao (Chapter 9)

    K¶

    Kingdom of ends
    Kant’s hypothetical, ideal society in which every individual is treated as an end and no one is treated as a means to an end. It is an idea that can be used to judge the morality of an action. (Chapter 8)
    Knowledge by acquaintance
    knowledge gained from direct contact with something and not mediated by inference. (Chapter 7)

    L¶

    Law of noncontradiction
    a logical law that states that contradictory statements/propositions can never be true in the same sense at the same time. (Chapter 5)
    Law of the excluded middle
    a logical law that states that for any statement, either that statement or its negation is true. (Chapter 5)
    Legitimacy
    in governance, acceptance of one’s right to rule by the people being ruled. (Chapter 11)
    Li
    rituals and practice that develop a person’s ethical character as they interact with others. (Chapter 3)
    Li
    ritual and practice that develop a person’s ethical character as they interact with others (Chapter 9)
    Libertarianism
    within the problem of freedom, the view that human actions are freely chosen and outside of the causality that governs natural objects. (Chapter 6)
    Linguistic turn
    a term used to signify a movement beginning in the early 20th century focusing on the philosophical value of verifiable, logically consistent statements as providing objective information about the universe; associated with analytic philosophy. (Chapter 12)
    Local skeptic
    someone who question the possibility of knowledge only in particular areas of study. (Chapter 7)
    Logic
    the formalization of reasoning. (Chapter 1)
    Logical analysis
    the process of determining whether the logical inferences made in an argument are good. A logical analysis determines whether the premises in an argument logically support the conclusion. (Chapter 5)
    Logos
    a way of thinking that rationally analyzes abstract concepts and phenomena independent of accepted belief systems. (Chapter 3)
    Logos
    the thoughts of God, which according to Philo of Alexandria serve as the means by which God creates the physical world. (Chapter 4)
    lower pleasures
    pleasures associated with the exercise of a person’s lower faculties (e.g., basic sensory pleasures) (Chapter 9)

    M¶

    Meaningful work
    work that is at the same time understood as an end and a possessor of moral status. (Chapter 10)
    Metacognition
    the process of thinking about thinking. Metacognition engages self-awareness and higher-order thinking skills so that an individual can regulate, monitor, and critically analyze their own thought processes. (Chapter 2)
    Metaethics
    branch of philosophy that focuses on moral reasoning and foundational questions that explore the assumptions related to moral beliefs and practice. (Chapter 8)
    metaethics
    a branch of ethics that focuses on foundational questions and moral reasoning (Chapter 9)
    Metaphysics
    the field of philosophy concerned with identifying that which is real. (Chapter 6)
    Milesians
    a school of early philosophers from Miletus; followers of Thales. They were known for examining the underlying causes of natural phenomena. (Chapter 1)
    Mohism
    a type of consequentialism established in ancient China by Mozi during the Warring States period. (Chapter 3)
    Mohism
    a type of consequentialism established in ancient China by Mozi (ca. 430 BCE) during the Warring States period (Chapter 9)
    Mohism
    the philosophy of the Chinese philosopher Mozi or the teachings of the Mozi, a book thought to be a collection of writings by followers of Mozi’s teachings. (Chapter 11)
    Monarchy
    a system of rule by one individual, who usually inherits their position. (Chapter 11)
    Monism
    the belief that the universe is made up of one substance. (Chapter 4)
    Monism
    the view that reality is comprised of one fundamental type of being. (Chapter 6)
    Monism
    theory that argues that there is only one fundamental intrinsic value that forms the foundation for all other values. (Chapter 8)
    Moral realism
    the philosophical position that morality is objective, not subjective. (Chapter 8)
    Moral relativism
    the philosophical position that there are multiple moral frameworks that are equally valid because values are relative to individuals, communities, and cultures. (Chapter 8)
    Moral skepticism
    the philosophical position that morality is not objective. (Chapter 8)
    Mythos
    a way of thinking that relies on the folk knowledge and narratives that often form part of the spiritual beliefs of a people. (Chapter 3)

    N¶

    Natural law
    moral law naturally intuited by humankind, according to the rationality given to them by God. (Chapter 11)
    Natural law theory
    an ethical position that asserts that morals are objective and derived from nature. (Chapter 8)
    Natural philosophy
    the fields of study that eventually gave rise to science. (Chapter 4)
    Naturalism
    the rejection of any non-natural or appeal to supernatural explanatory concepts within philosophy. (Chapter 6)
    naturalism
    a belief that ethical claims can be derived from nonethical ones (Chapter 9)
    Naturalistic fallacy
    an error in reasoning that assumes one can derive values (what people ought to do) from facts about the world (what is the case). (Chapter 8)
    Necessary condition
    X is a necessary condition for Y if and only if X must be true given the truth of Y. If X is necessary for Y, then X is guaranteed by Y—without the truth of X, Y cannot be true. (Chapter 5)
    Negative liberty
    a state in which one is neither constrained to act nor obligated to refrain from acting in a specific way. (Chapter 11)
    Non-cognitivism
    the philosophical position that values are not cognitive because they do not necessarily make statements about properties of things or states of events and have more to do with a psychological state of mind. (Chapter 8)
    normative ethics
    a branch of ethics that focuses on establishing norms and standards of moral conduct (Chapter 9)

    O¶

    Ontological argument
    an argument for the existence of God built upon a consideration of the attribute of God’s existence. (Chapter 6)
    Ontology
    a field within metaphysics dedicated to the study of being. (Chapter 6)
    Ontology of value
    the study of the being of values. (Chapter 8)
    Open-question argument
    G. E. Moore’s argument against the naturalistic fallacy, which he sees as trying to derive non-natural properties from natural properties. For Moore, arguing that something is “good” (a non-natural property) based on natural properties is circular and leaves an open question. (Chapter 8)
    Opportunity cost
    the cost incurred by not pursuing other options. (Chapter 10)

    P¶

    Particular
    when discussing being, the instance of a specific being. (Chapter 6)
    Passive euthanasia
    a form of euthanasia in which treatment is withheld or withdrawn with the expectation that a patient will die sooner than they would with continued medical intervention. (Chapter 10)
    perfect friendship
    relationships that foster individual virtue as they are based on love and the wish that another flourishes rather than the expectation of personal gain (Chapter 9)
    Perfectionism
    an approach to ethics that bases morality on the highest attainable good for an individual, human nature, or society. (Chapter 8)
    Personhood
    the capacity humans possess that distinguish them as beings capable of morality. (Chapter 10)
    Phenomenology
    the first-person study of how the “phenomena” of the world impact the consciousness, in contrast and response to philosophical schools of thought that start philosophical reflection with the realm of ideas. (Chapter 12)
    Philosophy
    the “love of wisdom.” An academic discipline that attempts to grasp the broadest possible understanding of things. It is characterized by rational explanation and a willingness to question assumptions. (Chapter 1)
    Physicalism
    the notion that being is material or physical. (Chapter 6)
    Physician-assisted suicide
    (PAS) a practice in which a physician provides the means (e.g., a prescription for a lethal dose of medication) and/or information to assist a patient in ending their own life. (Chapter 10)
    Pluralism
    asserts that fundamental reality consists of many types of being. (Chapter 6)
    Pluralism
    theory that argues that there are multiple fundamental intrinsic values rather than one. (Chapter 8)
    pluralism
    an approach to normative ethical theory that suggests a more complex, complete account of morality that provides for conflicting rules (Chapter 9)
    Plurism
    the belief that the universe is made up of more than one substance. (Chapter 4)
    Political philosophy
    the branch of philosophy that investigates concepts of justice and legitimacy as well as the relationships among political systems, governments, and the people. (Chapter 11)
    Positivism
    the third stage for the development of societies proposed by August Comte, in which people reject religion and focus only on things that can be proven. (Chapter 12)
    Post-structuralism
    views supporting the idea that the world cannot be interpreted through preexisting structures because there are no such existing structures; the idea that the universe is a confluence of forces that are given different meanings by human and nonhuman agents over time. (Chapter 12)
    Postmodernism
    the philosophical perspective that there is no absolute truth to the universe, leaving no grand objective narratives to categorize and structure the world (as in modernism) but everything to individual interpretation; the idea that truth is perspective. (Chapter 12)
    Potentiality
    in Aristotelian thought, the level to which a being’s purpose might reach. (Chapter 6)
    Prakriti
    in Hindu thought, matter; one of two elements that make up the universe. (Chapter 3)
    Predicate
    the portion of a sentence that provides the description or characterization of an object or name. (A philosophical predicate is different from the predicate of grammar, and their definitions should not be confused.) (Chapter 1)
    Premise
    evidence or a reason offered in support of a conclusion. (Chapter 5)
    Presentist approach
    an approach to philosophy that examines philosophical texts for the arguments they contain and judges how and whether they remain relevant today. (Chapter 4)
    prima facie duties
    duties that are obligatory, other things being equal, or so long as other factors and circumstances remain the same (Chapter 9)
    Principle of autonomy
    principle that states that patients have a right to exercise agency or self-determination when it comes to making decisions about their own health care in clinical settings. (Chapter 10)
    Principle of beneficence
    principle that states that we should act in ways that benefit others or that are for the good of others. (Chapter 10)
    Principle of charity
    the interpretative principle that says a reader ought to interpret the author’s statements in the most rational and best possible way. (Chapter 2)
    Principle of clinical equipoise
    principle that states that randomized trials should be conducted in a way that balances the interests of participants and aims of science. (Chapter 10)
    Principle of justice
    principle that states that the distribution and practice of health care should be equitable or fair. (Chapter 10)
    Principle of nonmaleficence
    principle that states that we should act in ways that do not cause harm to others. (Chapter 10)
    principle of utility
    a principle that holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote pleasure and diminish pain (Chapter 9)
    Procedural knowledge
    knowledge of how to successfully complete a task. (Chapter 7)
    Propositional knowledge
    knowledge of facts that can be expressed as statements. (Chapter 7)
    Psychoanalysis
    the attempt to cure mental illnesses by uncovering the unconscious elements that are said to be the foundation of human behavior. (Chapter 12)
    Purusha
    in Hindu thought, pure, absolute consciousness; one of two elements that make up the universe. (Chapter 3)

    R¶

    Realism
    the philosophical position that asserts that ethical values have some basis in reality and that reasoning about ethical matters requires an objective framework or foundation. (Chapter 8)
    Reason
    a methodical way of thinking that uses evidence and logic to draw conclusions, or the capacity to think this way. (Chapter 8)
    Red herring
    fallacy of diversion that ignores the opponent’s position and simply changes the subject. (Chapter 5)
    Reflective equilibrium
    a process of reviewing a theoretical position by going back and forth between the theory and its practical applications. This process seeks coherence between theory and practice. (Chapter 1)
    Ren
    a central concept in Confucianism that refers either to specific virtues or to someone with complete virtue. (Chapter 3)
    ren
    a central concept in Confucianism that is used to mean either someone with complete virtue or to refer to specific virtues (Chapter 9)
    Representation
    an information-bearing unit of thought. Representations are the objects that minds consider when they think. (Chapter 2)
    rule utilitarianism
    a utilitarian approach that proposes that people should use the greatest happiness principle to test possible moral rules to determine whether a given rule would produce greater happiness if it were followed (Chapter 9)

    S¶

    Sage
    a wise person. Many ancient cultures designated important wise figures as “sages.” (Chapter 1)
    Samkhya
    a dualist approach in Hindu metaphysics that views the universe as composed of pure consciousness and matter, which undergoes an evolutionary process. (Chapter 3)
    Sanatana dharma
    the core or absolute set of moral and religious duties ordained for all people of ancient India, regardless of class or caste, and that predate the term Hinduism. (Chapter 1)
    Satisfactionism
    a philosophical position that defines well-being as satisfying desires. (Chapter 8)
    Self-criticism
    term for a method of public self-analysis proposed by Mao Tse-Tung as a means to achieve personal and societal improvement. (Chapter 12)
    Semiotics
    an analysis of how meaning is created through symbols, both linguistic and nonlinguistic. (Chapter 12)
    Shareholders
    individuals who own a share of a corporation. (Chapter 10)
    Skepticism
    a philosophical position that claims people do not know things they ordinarily think they know. (Chapter 3)
    Skepticism
    the view that some or all knowledge is impossible. (Chapter 7)
    skepticism
    a philosophical position that claims people do not know things they ordinarily think they know (Chapter 9)
    Social contract
    an agreement among members of society to cooperate and allow some limits of their natural rights in exchange for protection and mutual benefits provided by government. (Chapter 11)
    Socratic method
    a method of questioning used by Socrates (and named after him later) to help people understand what they were thinking and to arrive at some truth. (Chapter 1)
    Somatic cell interventions
    genetic interventions in which genetic changes cannot be inherited or passed to a patient’s offspring. (Chapter 10)
    Stakeholders
    any individual who has a stake in a business’s operations. (Chapter 10)
    Standpoint epistemology
    study of the relationship between an individual’s social status and their epistemic position. (Chapter 7)
    Statement
    a sentence with a truth value—a sentence that must be either true or false. (Chapter 5)
    Statement
    A declarative sentence that has a truth value, meaning that it must be either true or false. (Chapter 7)
    Steelmanning
    a strategy for making opposing arguments as strong as possible so that it is difficult to knock them down. (Chapter 2)
    Strawman
    fallacy of diversion that utilizes a weaker version of the position being argued against in order to make the position easier to defeat. (Chapter 5)
    Strong artificial intelligence
    machines that perform multiple cognitive tasks like humans but at a very rapid pace (machine speed). (Chapter 10)
    Structuralism
    the belief that the universe has a certain objective structure to it and that language indicates this structure; the belief that in order to understand individual parts of the universe, one must understand their place in the overarching structure of things. (Chapter 12)
    Substance
    the most enduring and underlying reality of a thing; from the Latin substantiaI or that which supports a thing. (Chapter 6)
    Sufficient condition
    X is a sufficient condition for Y if and only if the truth of X guarantees the truth of Y. If X is sufficient for Y, then the truth of X is enough to prove the truth of Y. (Chapter 5)
    Sunk-cost fallacy
    the fallacy of attaching a greater value to something than is warranted because a person has already invested time, resources, and emotion in that thing (or person). (Chapter 2)

    T¶

    Teleological argument
    an argument for the existence of God based upon the presence of ends (goals or purpose) as observed within nature. (Chapter 6)
    Telos
    the purpose, end, or goal of something. (Chapter 8)
    Testimonial injustice
    a type of epistemic injustice that occurs when the opinions of individuals or groups are unfairly ignored or treated as untrustworthy. (Chapter 7)
    Theory of the forms
    a metaphysical doctrine that holds that every particular thing that exists in our changing, material world participates in an immaterial form or essence, which is unchanging, invisible, and perfect and which gives this thing its identity. (Chapter 4)
    Thought experiment
    an imaginative scenario that tests some philosophical theory or concept by considering how it might apply in the imagined situation. (Chapter 1)
    Totalitarianism
    a system of government that exercises complete control over its people in terms of both their personal and their public lives. (Chapter 11)
    Transformative model of identity
    an understanding of social identity as spiraling both outward and inward through expanding and retracting influences over a certain area of land. (Chapter 3)
    Tribalism
    the tendency for human beings to align their beliefs and attitudes with groups of people who have similar attitudes, practices, or beliefs. (Chapter 2)
    trolley problems
    classic thought experiments that use difficult ethical dilemmas to examine moral reasoning and deliberation (Chapter 9)
    Truth analysis
    the process of determining whether statements made in an argument are either true or false. (Chapter 5)

    U¶

    Universal
    when discussing being, a reality or concept that accounts for the shared whatness of a specific type of being. (Chapter 6)
    Universal affirmative statement
    statements that take two groups of things and claim all members of the first group are also members of the second group. (Chapter 5)
    universal law formulation
    a rational method of devising moral laws that proposes that a moral law must be applied universally to the whole of society (Chapter 9)
    Upanishads
    Hindu texts that contain the philosophical core of Hinduism. (Chapter 3)
    Utilitarianism
    an ethical theory that bases morality on maximizing pleasure and minimizing pain. (Chapter 8)
    utilitarianism
    a type of consequentialism introduced by Jeremy Bentham and developed by John Stuart Mill (Chapter 9)

    V¶

    Validity
    a property of deductive arguments where the structure of an argument is such that if the premises are true, then the conclusion is guaranteed to be true. A valid inference is a logically good inference. (Chapter 5)
    Value theory
    the philosophical investigation of values. In its narrow sense, it refers to metaethical concerns. In its broader sense, it addresses a variety of values (ethical, social, political, religious, aesthetic, etc.) (Chapter 8)
    Values
    beliefs and evaluations about morality, politics, aesthetics, and social issues. They often express a judgment about what people think ought to be the case. (Chapter 8)
    Vedas
    the four oldest books within Hinduism, consisting of the Rigveda, the Samaveda, the Yajurveda, and the Atharvaveda. (Chapter 3)
    Veil of ignorance
    an imagined scenario in which a person deliberately remains unaware of any personal traits and does not know what social, political, or economic group they are in. (Chapter 11)
    Virtue ethics
    an approach to normative ethics that focuses on character. (Chapter 3)
    Virtue ethics
    a philosophical approach to ethics based on the examination of different virtues. (Chapter 8)
    virtue ethics
    an approach to normative ethics that focuses on character (Chapter 9)

    W¶

    Warring States period
    a period of widespread conflict, suffering, and social unrest in Chinese history that gave rise to highly influential philosophical approaches, including Mohism, Confucianism, and Daoism (Chapter 9)
    Weak artificial intelligence
    machines that perform primarily one task, such as Apple’s Siri or social media bots. (Chapter 10)
    Well being
    concept referring to what is good for a person, not simply what is good in an abstract sense. (Chapter 8)
    wu wei
    a natural way of acting—also called nonaction—that is spontaneous or immediate, one in which a person’s actions are in harmony with the flow of nature or existence (Chapter 9)
    Wuwei
    a natural way of acting that is spontaneous or immediate, in which a person’s actions are in harmony with the flow of nature or existence. (Chapter 3)

    Y¶

    Yin and yang
    an explanation of natural phenomena through two fundamental forces, the male yin and the female yang. (Chapter 3)

    Z¶

    Zeno’s paradoxes
    paradoxes proposed by Zeno that attempt to prove that change and motion are illusory. (Chapter 4)
